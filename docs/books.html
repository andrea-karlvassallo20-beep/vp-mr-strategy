Part 1 — The microstructure stack: how the book is the market

1. Larry Harris, Trading and Exchanges (2002)

What it is: the encyclopedia of how markets actually work — who trades, why, and what happens mechanically when they do. Harris starts where our Part 0 starts: an order is either patient (a limit order that waits in the book) or impatient (an aggressor that crosses the spread). Everything else in the book is built from that one split.

What it teaches, from zero: the limit order book is a queue, and queues have physics. The spread is the price of immediacy — the toll impatience pays patience. Market makers are not villains; they are suppliers of immediacy who earn the toll and manage the risk of being picked off by someone better informed. Orders arrive, rest, cancel, and execute according to rules the exchange publishes; price is whatever the last meeting printed. Harris also teaches the taxonomy of traders — informed, uninformed, liquidity-motivated — and the game between them: the informed want to hide, the market-makers want to detect, and the spread itself is the detector's fee.

Applied here: the entire event-clock design (state-change rows, self-thinning), the aggressor reconstruction (one bit per trade, coercion failure kills the file), and the verification gate's logic — a book that is verified before it is believed — are Harris's mechanics turned into code. The C5/C6 claims (absorption asymmetry, depth OFI) are his order-flow economics. BLOCKED ON FULL-DEPTH L2

2. Joel Hasbrouck, Empirical Market Microstructure (2004)

What it is: the statistics of the book. Where Harris describes the machinery, Hasbrouck measures it: how to take the stream of trades and quotes and extract what the flow reveals about value.

What it teaches, from zero: a price is a noisy measurement of value. The trade you observe = true value + noise from the bid-ask bounce + the footprint of whoever was impatient. His signature tools: the Roll model (you can estimate the spread from prices alone, because the bounce leaves a signature in the autocovariance), and VAR decompositions (split price changes into a permanent part — what flow revealed — and a transitory part — the bounce that settles back). The deep lesson: information does not arrive as announcements; it arrives as order flow, and prices are the running average of what the flow has revealed.

Applied here: the design rule that direction, if it ever exists, must come from flow rather than price — the empirical backbone of the 0.505 boundary's interpretation — and the permanent/transitory split that underlies the short-horizon mean-reversion half of lesson 3. The C8 covariate design (primed registry as lagged time-varying covariates) is Hasbrouck's "flow reveals, price remembers" logic in a Cox regression. BINDING

3. Biais, Glosten & Spatt, Market Microstructure: A Survey (~2005)

What it is: the unified theory. The classic microstructure papers (Glosten-Milgrom, Kyle, and successors) each modeled one corner of the problem; this survey builds one synthetic model that contains them all.

What it teaches, from zero: three forces shape every spread. Adverse selection: the fear that the person trading against you knows more (Glosten-Milgrom) — the market-maker widens the spread as insurance against the informed. Inventory: a dealer who has absorbed too much of one side must discount to rebalance (Ho-Stoll) — a purely mechanical, temporary pressure. Strategic interaction: large traders split orders and hide, precisely because showing size moves the price against them (Kyle) — which is why one decision becomes a burst of trades (lesson 5). The survey's gift is showing these are not competing theories but three terms of one equation.

Applied here: the three-term split is the reason the programme separates toxicity signals (VPIN — adverse selection) from absorption signals (effort-vs-result — inventory/impact) instead of lumping them into one "flow" number. SHAPES C5/C6 DESIGN

4. Bouchaud, Bonart, Donier & Gould, Trades, Quotes and Prices (2018) PARTIAL — CH. 1–2.1.4

What it is: the econophysics of the order book — markets studied as collective systems of many agents, the way physicists study sandpiles and magnets. Studied only in part; the chapters that matter most (Hawkes processes, queue models, price-impact laws) are still in the queue.

What it teaches, from zero (in the chapters studied): price changes are not smooth; they are the sum of discrete book events, and the statistics of those events — heavy tails, clustering, concave impact — are emergent properties of queues, not choices of any participant. No one "decides" the square-root law; it emerges from the geometry of waiting liquidity (lesson 4's two-effects fight). The book's whole stance: stop modeling the representative agent; count the events.

Applied here: the event clock, the Hawkes excitation matrix in the L2 layer, and the cancellation-fraction kill switch in the acquisition spec — a frozen book makes Hawkes meaningless, a kill switch the spec took directly from this book's logic. REQUIRED READING BEFORE C5/C6 RUN — the programme refuses to test claims whose foundational chapters it has not studied.

5. Foucault, Pagano & Röell, Market Liquidity (2013)

What it is: the theory of liquidity itself — what makes some books deep and others empty, and how market design (tick sizes, fees, transparency) changes it.

What it teaches, from zero: liquidity is not one number. It is the spread (the toll), the depth (how much waits), and the resilience (how fast the book refills after being hit). The book develops Kyle's lambda formally: the coefficient that converts net order flow into price change — the market's friction coefficient (lesson 28). It also develops PIN, the probability that the counterparty to any given trade is informed — the theoretical parent of VPIN (lesson 25). And it teaches market design: the same traders under different rules produce different books, which is why a verification gate must check the recorded book against itself before any analysis is legal.

Applied here: lambda as the C8 absorption co-reading (OFI absorption with declining lambda), VPIN's theoretical footing, and the gate's depth-consistency test. BLOCKED ON FULL-DEPTH L2

6. Stoll, The Microstructure of Trading survey (2002)

What it is: the founder's retrospective — the man who first decomposed the spread into its sources, looking back over the field he helped create.

What it teaches, from zero: the spread is not one toll but five smaller ones stacked: order-processing cost (the clerk's fee), adverse-selection risk (insurance against the informed), inventory risk (the cost of holding the wrong side), monopoly rent (competition squeezes this one), and the search cost of finding the other side. Each source leaves a different fingerprint in the time series — which is the practical gift: you can measure which component dominates a market's spread by watching how prices behave after trades.

Applied here: the spread-decomposition logic behind separating toxicity from absorption (with Biais et al.), and the persistence of the "fingerprint" idea in how the programme reads volume signatures before moves. BINDING

7. Madhavan, Market Microstructure: A Survey (2000)

What it is: the information-economics survey — what happens to prices when participants hold different information.

What it teaches, from zero: the single most durable sentence in the stack: information effects are permanent; inventory effects are transitory. When an informed trader buys, the price rises and stays risen — the flow revealed something true. When a dealer rebalances inventory, the price moves and settles back — nothing was revealed. This one sentence is the entire basis for reading order flow as information rather than noise. Madhavan also surveys transparency: showing everyone the book redistributes who wins, which is why hidden liquidity exists and why flicker (lesson 30) is a strategy, not an accident.

Applied here: the binding design rule that direction, if it ever exists, must come from flow, not price — permanent effects live in flow; the price only remembers. BINDING

Part 2 — The practitioner and history layers

8. Tom Williams, Master the Markets (VSA, 2009)

What it is: the practitioner's dictionary of reading price and volume together — Volume Spread Analysis, descended from Richard Wyckoff's 1930s work. Written by a syndicate trader; the polar opposite of the academic stack, and read precisely for that reason.

What it teaches, from zero: a chart shows two quantities — price (the result) and volume (the effort). The signals are all ratios of the two. Absorption: huge volume, no progress — someone big is quietly feeding a patient crowd. Effort with no result: high volume into a level that refuses to break — the level is defended. Upthrusts and shake-outs: quick probes through a level that instantly reverse — designed to trigger stops and transfer inventory from the weak to the strong. No demand: rallies on shrinking volume — nobody believes. The whole book is lesson 4 (effort vs. result) with names.

Applied here: effort-vs-result is a measured column in every scan (evr), the absorption signature is one of the two C8 trigger co-readings, and the upthrust/shake-out dictionary became claims C2 and C3 — which had to be operationalized into breach-and-recover with fixed parameters before they could even be declared, and which died with the daily program. The teacher's signal survived; the teacher's timing did not. C2 DEAD BY DEPENDENCY · C3 CLOSED

9. Scott Patterson, The Quants

What it is: the history of the quant era — how mathematics took over Wall Street, and what happened when the machines met 1987, August 2007, and 2008.

What it teaches, from zero: every quant disaster shares one shape: a model that worked on history, leveraged hard, meeting a day history had not contained. The lesson is not "quants bad"; it is the model is not the market — the map omits the territory's rare days, and leverage converts omissions into bankruptcies. Patterson's chapters on the 2007 quant quake (crowded positions unwinding with no news) teach that risk can be endogenous: positions themselves, not information, can move markets.

Applied here: the refusal to trust simulated profitability (the design-sample flag), the CVaR/drawdown constraint on all sizing, and the refusal to size on a wide posterior — all downstream of this book. BINDING ON ALL SIZING

10. Scott Patterson, Dark Pools

What it is: the history of the venues themselves — how trading migrated from floors to hidden electronic pools, and the anatomy of the Flash Crash along the way.

What it teaches, from zero: the May 2010 Flash Crash in one sentence: liquidity is a promise, not a guarantee — when the algorithmic quotes withdrew, the book emptied in seconds and prices went to absurd places before recovering. Two durable ideas: quote withdrawal is a strategy (flicker, lesson 30, is its everyday form), and feedback loops (market-makers' algorithms watching each other, all reacting to the same signal, all exiting at once) are how a modern crash propagates — no humans, no news, just loops. Fat tails are not exotic; they are what loops produce.

Applied here: the cancellation/trade distinction at the heart of the verification gate, and the treatment of flicker as strategy rather than noise. BINDING

Part 3 — The mathematical-finance core

11. Tomas Björk, Arbitrage Theory in Continuous Time

What it is: the mathematical spine of derivative pricing: no-arbitrage, martingales, and the two Fundamental Theorems of asset pricing.

What it teaches, from zero: a martingale is a process whose best guess of tomorrow is today — the mathematical form of "no free prediction." The First Fundamental Theorem says: if markets permit no arbitrage, prices (properly discounted) must be martingales — which is why lesson 9's 0.505-with-CI-containing-0.50 is not a disappointment but the theorem holding. The Second says the market prices every state. From these follows Black-Scholes — not as a description of reality, but as the price consistency demands if volatility were constant, which it is not (lesson 1), which is precisely why volatility itself is the thing to trade. Björk also covers Merton's portfolio problem — the ancestor of Kelly-style sizing.

Applied here: the martingale reading of the 0.505 boundary, the convexity economics (why a directionless convex payoff is the honest instrument for a WHAT claim), and the quarter-Kelly sizing discipline. BINDING

12. Alireza Javaheri, Inside Volatility Arbitrage

What it is: the practical zoo of volatility models — GARCH, stochastic-vol, jump-diffusion — and, more importantly, which of their parameters can actually be estimated from data.

What it teaches, from zero: a volatility model has a handful of parameters; the market gives you one noisy series to estimate them from; and at daily frequency, most of the parameters are not identifiable — the data cannot tell them apart. Fit the noise-parameters anyway and you get a model that describes the past beautifully and predicts nothing. The identification hierarchy — which parameters daily data can see (persistence), which it cannot (mean-reversion speed, correlation of vol shocks) — is the book's durable gift.

Applied here: the estimation discipline: the lab refuses to fit noise, which is why C1 was specified with the fewest parameters that could express the claim, and why every descriptive scan reports percentiles instead of fitted curves. BINDING

13. Levy, Computational Finance

What it is: numerical pricing plus GARCH econometrics — the book where fat tails are derived rather than observed.

What it teaches, from zero: feed independent Gaussian shocks through a volatility process with persistence (today's vol depends on yesterday's) and the output is already heavy-tailed — no exotic ingredients required. Clustering plus persistence manufactures tails; lesson 1 and lesson 2 are one mechanism, not two. The book's S&P identification result shows the persistence condition holding on decades of real data — the reason volatility clustering is the most reliable stylized fact in finance.

Applied here: the theoretical grounding of lessons 1–2 (clustering and tails as one machine), and the GARCH-family logic behind the rvol percentiles every scan uses. BINDING

Part 4 — The base framework and its honest supersession

14. The Elliott Wave framework (the starting point)

What it is: the first framework the programme learned — markets move in motive waves and corrective waves, five and three, fractally at every scale. It was the base of the original AVFI-NQ weekly research, and the lens through which the first 20 books were read.

What it taught, and what happened: the wave framework's honest core is that markets alternate between trending and consolidating phases — the observation that survives as lesson 3 and the coil. Its weakness is the one the programme measured: wave counting is not falsifiable (two counters disagree on the same chart, and no kill line can adjudicate), and the AVFI programme built on it was killed by its own validation pipeline — rule set A Sharpe −0.115 in-sample, the grid family failing the White reality check, every AVFI variant failing individually. The programme's own verdict: a slow regime descriptor, not a weekly timer.

Applied here: superseded, not erased — the SMH charter keeps the durable part (markets breathe between trend and consolidation) and replaces the unfalsifiable part (wave counting) with measured states (the coil metrics) and pre-declared kill lines. The AVFI page itself was removed by directive and replaced by the heat map. SUPERSEDED · ITS LESSONS BIND

15. Applied Quantitative Finance (the applied theory toolkit)

What it is: the bridge-volume between the pure theory of Björk and the desk: how theoretical prices become implementable numbers under real constraints — transaction costs, discrete hedging, model error, and calibration that must run before the market moves.

What it teaches, from zero: a model price is a limiting case; a trade is a discrete, costly approximation of it. The book's core discipline is the gap analysis: every real position differs from the theoretical one by transaction costs (proportional and, crucially, impact — the square-root law again), by discrete rebalancing (hedge error accumulates like a random walk between rebalance dates), and by parameter error (your σ is estimated, not known). Its second gift is calibration: fitting a model to market instruments means inverting it — searching parameter space for the values that reproduce observed prices — which is why the optimization book ends this queue (you cannot calibrate what you cannot optimize).

Applied here: the convexity economics is exactly this book's discipline — the refusal rule and CVaR cap exist because the theoretical payoff and the held position differ by theta, spread, and impact. The calibration mindset (invert, never assume) is the ancestor of the CPCV philosophy: a model is fit by searching, and the search must be honest. BINDING

16. Tsay, Analysis of Financial Time Series

What it is: the econometrics of financial series — the book that teaches how to speak about time itself in statistics.

What it teaches, from zero: a financial series is not a bag of numbers; it has structure in time, and the book builds the toolkit layer by layer. ARMA: today's value depends on yesterday's value and yesterday's noise — the linear memory of a series. GARCH: today's volatility depends on yesterday's volatility and yesterday's shock — the formal statement of lesson 1 (clustering) with the persistence condition Levy grounded. Heavy tails and nonlinearity: the zoo of threshold models and Markov switching — regimes exist, and pretending they don't smears everything into one wrong average. Realized volatility: when you have intraday data, volatility stops being a modeled parameter and becomes a measurement — the ancestor of everything the event clock computes. And the multivariate chapters: cointegration — two series can wander individually yet hold a stable relationship, the statistical home of basis and spread trades.

Applied here: the rvol percentiles in every scan are Tsay's realized-volatility logic at daily grain; the basis-extreme frustration metric is a cointegration-flavored reading of perp vs spot; and the L2 phase's realized measures are this book's high-frequency chapters made operational. The GARCH persistence condition is why the programme reports vol states rather than fitting vol curves. BINDING

17. Elliott & Kopp, Mathematics of Financial Markets

What it is: the discrete-time foundations — the same Fundamental Theorems as Björk but built on trees and coin tosses first, where nothing can hide behind continuous-time algebra.

What it teaches, from zero: take the simplest possible market — two states tomorrow, one asset, one bond. Ask: which prices today are consistent with no free lunch? The answer forces the existence of state prices: weights under which every asset's expected value equals its price — the discrete martingale measure. Then generalize: many periods, many states, and the same theorem keeps holding. The book's second half faces what Björk mostly avoids: incomplete markets — when states cannot all be hedged, there is no unique price, only bounds, and pricing becomes a choice of measure. That is not a defect; it is the truth about most real contingent claims.

Applied here: the state-price logic is why the programme prices nothing it cannot hedge and why the convexity economics insists on bounds (the lower CPCV bound) instead of point estimates — incomplete markets give intervals, and honesty is pricing the interval. BINDING

18. The algorithmic/HFT books (the execution layer)

What they are: the practitioner stack between statistics and the wire: how strategies become orders, and what the machines do to each other in the microseconds between.

What they teach, from zero: execution is a problem of hiding size inside liquidity. A parent order is sliced into child orders (VWAP/TWAP schedules, implementation-shortfall controllers) precisely because of the square-root law — everything lesson 4 says about impact is the reason this industry exists. At the fast end, the taxonomy of HFT strategies is a tour of the book's physics: market-making (earn the spread, manage adverse selection), latency arbitrage (the book as a race), statistical signaling (read the flow before the mid moves), and momentum ignition (trigger the cascades lesson 5 describes — legal controversy included). Two durable warnings live here: the latency game decays faster than any statistical edge (lesson 6 at machine speed), and queue position is an asset — being first at a price level is worth money, which is why cancellations and re-posts are strategic (lesson 30) and why the gate must tell trades from flicker.

Applied here: the entire event-clock design assumes the world these books describe — bursts, slicing, flicker, and races. The cancellation-fraction kill switch in the acquisition spec is this layer's warning written as a kill line: a frozen or flickering book makes every flow feature a lie. BINDING ON THE GATE

19. Nonlinear Optimization (the engine under everything)

What it is: the mathematics of finding the best when the best is not a formula — the machine every calibration, every MLE, and every sizing decision secretly runs on.

What it teaches, from zero: an optimization problem is three things: a feasible set (what you're allowed to do), an objective (what you're minimizing), and the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions — the equations that hold at any best point: the gradient of the objective must be a weighted combination of the active constraints' gradients. If nothing constrains you, the gradient is zero; if something does, the constraint pushes back exactly as hard as it costs. The book then teaches which problems are honest: convex ones (bowl-shaped objective) have one best point and every local answer is global; non-convex ones have many valleys and every search is a confession of heuristics — which is why the dry run's Hawkes estimator carries a Nelder–Mead fallback with bounded log-parameters: a non-convex search, honestly bounded so it cannot wander into absurd parameter country.

Applied here: the Hawkes MLE, the Cox partial likelihood, the quarter-Kelly sizing under CVaR (a convex problem — the reason its answer can be trusted), and the calibration mindset of book 15 are all this book's territory. The programme's rule follows directly: know which of your searches is convex before you trust its answer. BINDING

20. Bouchaud et al., Trades, Quotes and Prices — the Hawkes and queue chapters (the required reading, taught in full)

What they are: the two chapters the programme refuses to run C5/C6 without — the econophysics of timing (Hawkes) and of the book itself (queues). Studied now from standard knowledge; the text remains the authority.

The Hawkes chapters, from zero: a Poisson process says events forget each other; real markets don't. The Hawkes process gives every event a voice: when an event happens, the arrival rate jumps up by a fixed amount and decays exponentially. The mathematics condenses into the excitation matrix: entry (i,j) asks how many events of type j an event of type i tends to cause. Sum an event's row and you get its branching ratio — the expected number of children. The deep result: the process is stable if and only if the spectral radius (the largest eigenvalue of the excitation matrix) is below one. As the radius approaches one, the market approaches criticality: cascades that barely die out, clusters that grow, a system one nudge from self-sustaining storm. This is precisely the C8 trigger signature — "Hawkes excitation going critical" is the spectral radius crossing a threshold, estimated on the event clock, with the branching structure estimated across buy-aggressive and sell-aggressive event types separately.

The queue chapters, from zero: the limit order book is a queueing system — arrivals (limit orders), service (executions), and abandonment (cancellations). Queueing theory's central question — when does a queue explode? — becomes the book's central measurement: the cancellation fraction, the share of orders that leave without trading. A healthy book has arrivals balanced by executions and cancellations; a frozen or gaming book has pathological cancellation dynamics, and every flow feature computed on it is a lie told by the data. The chapters also develop the propagator model of impact: the price move after a trade is the sum of a permanent shift (information, per Madhavan) and a transient response that decays as the book refills (liquidity, per lesson 23's resilience) — the microstructure form of Hasbrouck's permanent/transitory decomposition, and the formal basis for reading absorption: flow whose propagator response decays while lambda declines is flow being fed by a patient book, not flow moving a thin one.

Applied here: these chapters ARE the C5/C6 specification: the Hawkes excitation matrix in layer 2, the cancellation-fraction kill switch in the acquisition spec, the propagator reading behind the absorption-asymmetry claim, and the criticality threshold behind the first trigger signature. The programme's refusal to run these claims before studying the actual chapters — and before the gate verifies the recorded book — is the same rule as refusing OFI on synthetic frames: no claim outruns its foundations.
