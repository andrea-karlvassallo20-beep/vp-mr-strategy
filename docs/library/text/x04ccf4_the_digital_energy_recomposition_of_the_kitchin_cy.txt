Abstract
Using quarterly FRED and CSO Ireland data over 1995Q1-2025Q4, we examine
whether U.S. IT investment and Irish data-centre electricity are recomposing the
Kitchin cycle around energy- and compute-backed assets. Three findings stand out.
First, a Quandt-Andrews sup-Wald test locates a significant break in the U.S. IT-
GDP correlation at 2014Q3 (sup-W = 9.69; r falls from +0.45 to −0.12); the Fisher
r-to-z test survives Bonferroni adjustment (z = +3.11, pBonf = 0.006), whereas the
analogous test on the imposed Cloud-vs-AI boundary does not (pBonf = 0.164), so
“Strategic Decoupling” pre-dates the AI Era by approximately six years. Second, in
Ireland the synchronous AI-Era correlation between metered data-centre electricity
and Real GDP is large (r = +0.41, N = 16) but collapses to r ≈ 0 when Real
GDP is replaced by Real GNP, suggesting the headline is largely an artefact of the
shared multinational tax-structure footprint between hyperscaler operations and Irish
gross output. Third, the Irish cross-correlation profile is asymmetric in the opposite
direction to a leading-indicator reading: the largest positive coefficients cluster on the
k < 0 side (GDP precedes data-centre load), so compute capacity is better read as
coincident or moderately lagging. Given the small AI-Era samples and the absence
of a quarterly multinational-adjusted aggregate such as GNI*, all coefficients are
exploratory; subject to these caveats, the U.S. Kitchin cycle is best dated to 2014Q3,
and the Irish Energy-Data Nexus is dominated by multinational firm activity.
Keywords: Strategic Decoupling, Data Center Energy, AI Infrastructure, Economic Peri-
odicity, Lead-Lag Analysis, Energy-GDP Nexus.

1 Introduction
The study of economic periodicity has long been a cornerstone of macroeconomic forecasting
and investment strategy. Among the established wave theories, the Kitchin cycle, first
identified by Joseph Kitchin in 1923, remains one of the most influential frameworks for
understanding short/medium-term economic fluctuations. In his seminal work, “Cycles and
Trends in Economic Factors”, Kitchin observed a minor cycle of approximately 40 months
(3-5 years), driven by fluctuations in commercial inventories and capital investment.
For nearly a century, the Kitchin cycle served as a reference point for asset allocation and
production planning. It operated on a simple feedback loop: a surge in demand led to an
accumulation of physical inventory (e.g., steel, coal, finished goods), eventually resulting in
market saturation, price corrections, and subsequent inventory liquidation. This “inventory
lag” provided a predictable rhythm that investors used to time market entries and exits.
However, the structural realities of the 21st-century global economy differ from the
industrial landscape of the 1920s. We argue that the traditional Kitchin cycle, while
theoretically elegant, may be losing relevance in its original form. The transition from a
manufacturing-based economy to a digital-first infrastructure plausibly shifts the relevant
“inventory” from physical warehouses to digital ones, specifically to compute power,
semiconductor stockpiles, and energy contracts.
This paper introduces a revised framework: the “New Energy and Data Cycle.”
We document an empirical “Strategic Decoupling” of U.S. IT investment from GDP, and
ask when, on the data, it begins. While our nominal AI Era window is 2021Q1-2025Q4, a
Quandt-Andrews sup-Wald test on the U.S. IT-GDP correlation locates the structural break
in 2014Q3, several years before the current wave of AI investment; the corresponding Fisher
r-to-z test on the pre/post-2014Q3 sub-samples survives Bonferroni adjustment (z = +3.11,
pBonf = 0.006), in contrast to the analogous test on the imposed Cloud-vs-AI era boundary
(pBonf = 0.164). The pattern we report is therefore best read as exploratory evidence
consistent with a longer-running decoupling of IT capital formation from GDP, of which
the AI Era is the latest segment, rather than as a phenomenon uniquely attributable to AI.
In parallel, we examine whether energy consumption in data centers and semiconductor
manufacturing behaves as a coincident or leading correlate of growth in the AI Era window.
Using quarterly data from the Republic of Ireland, a country with a globally significant
share of data-center capacity, we examine whether the modern Kitchin cycle is better
explained by the availability and consumption of electricity than by physical goods. We
acknowledge that Irish GDP statistics are heavily influenced by multinational firm activity
(FitzGerald, 2018), a well-documented limitation revisited in Section 7; accordingly, the
Irish evidence is presented as an illustrative, not a confirmatory, case study. Our results
suggest that power purchase agreements (PPAs) and data center load deserve consideration
alongside traditional stock-to-sales ratios as candidate barometers of economic health in
data-intensive economies.
2 Literature Review
The study of economic periodicity has long been anchored in the framework established
by Kitchin (1923), who identified a “minor” cycle of approximately 40 months (3-5 years)
driven by fluctuations in commercial inventories and capital investment. Schumpeter (1939)

subsequently embedded the Kitchin cycle into a broader synthesis of short, medium (Juglar)
and long (Kondratiev) waves, and Burns and Mitchell (1946) provided the methodologi-
cal foundation for measuring such cycles empirically via reference-cycle dating and the
inventory-to-sales ratio. Traditionally, this cycle operated on a feedback loop where de-
mand surges led to the accumulation of physical stocks, such as steel and coal, eventually
resulting in market saturation and liquidation. However, as the global economy transitions
toward a digital-first infrastructure, the “inventory” at the heart of this cycle plausibly
shifts from physical warehouses to digital ones, encompassing compute power and energy
contracts.
The traditional Kitchin framework treats energy as a near-perfectly elastic input. The
“Energy-GDP Nexus” literature suggests, by contrast, that in high-tech economies, energy
constraints can dictate the upper bounds of the cycle (Stern, 2011). As data centers evolve
into a key element of digital industrial capacity, electricity consumption may shift from a
coincident toward a leading role. The empirical literature on data-centre energy use is dom-
inated by Koomey (2008), which documented the doubling of global data-centre electricity
demand between 2000 and 2005, and Masanet et al. (2020), which recalibrated the global
data-centre energy estimate to about 200 TWh per year despite an order-of-magnitude
increase in compute, by isolating efficiency gains in hyperscaler facilities. The Interna-
tional Energy Agency (2024) Electricity 2024 report extends this trajectory through 2026,
projecting that AI-driven workloads, cryptocurrency, and data-centre cooling will roughly
double global data-centre electricity demand within two years. These works frame our use
of metered data-centre consumption as a candidate macroeconomic indicator: the ques-
tion is whether the country-level cycle picks up the structural growth that these aggregate
studies document.
This potential structural break can be understood through the lens of technological
revolutions. Perez (2002) describes how financial capital and technological shifts create
structural changes in economic rhythms, a pattern arguably mirrored in the current transi-
tion into the AI era. Consequently, the nature of capital itself appears to have changed: as
Haskel and Westlake (2017) argue, the rise of the intangible economy has decoupled growth
from physical assets, making traditional inventory metrics less informative.
A critical component of this dematerialized inventory is the semiconductor sector. Un-
like the 1920s steel inventories, the “Silicon Cycle” is characterized by extreme capital
intensity and long lead times. This inherent lag is one possible explanation for the “Strate-
gic Decoupling” observed in recent data; semiconductor manufacturing often follows a
4-to-5 year investment rhythm (Juglar-like) that overlaps and occasionally disrupts the
shorter Kitchin cycle. The negative correlation observed between 2021 and 2025 may thus
represent a “pre-emptive investment phase,” in which firms accumulate compute capacity
largely independently of current GDP fluctuations to hedge against future supply shocks.
The shift toward AI-driven utility also calls for a re-evaluation of investment behavior
through the lens of Real Options Theory (Dixit and Pindyck, 1994). From this perspective,
IT investment in the AI era is not a reaction to current output, but a strategic “option” on
future productivity. This is consistent with the observation that, despite macroeconomic
headwinds, investment in GPU clusters and energy-backed infrastructure has remained
resilient. The decoupling of growth from physical assets emphasised by Haskel and Westlake
(2017) is thus arguably extended by the virtualization of the inventory itself: from stockpiles
of goods to stockpiles of “potential computation.”

The challenge of measuring such cycles in real time is addressed in the methodology of
Aruoba et al. (2009), whose work emphasises the use of high-frequency data to monitor
business conditions, providing a statistical precedent for using non-traditional indicators
like electricity consumption; recent work on large language models in financial analysis
(Zi´o lko et al., 2026) is auxiliary to the energy-centred argument advanced here.
Taken together, the literature motivates the hypothesis that the Kitchin cycle has not
disappeared but is being recomposed: traditional IT investment is increasingly decoupled
from contemporaneous GDP, while semiconductor stockpiling and data-center capacity
behave more like strategic, anti-cyclical assets. The analysis that follows is intended to
probe, rather than confirm, this hypothesis.
3 Methodology
The core objective of this study is to examine the structural shift in the Kitchin cycle,
moving from a traditional inventory-based model to a digital-energy framework. We em-
ploy a multi-stage quantitative approach to analyze the relationship between macroeco-
nomic growth and technological infrastructure. The ex ante “Digital-Energy Kitchin Cy-
cle” framework underlying the empirical strategy below is reported as Figure S1 in the
Supplementary Materials; the analysis that follows tests, rather than assumes, that frame-
work.
3.1 Analytical Framework
We segment the historical data into distinct technological eras (PC Era, Dot-com Boom,
Web 2.0, Cloud Expansion, and the AI Era beginning 2021Q1) to examine whether cor-
relation patterns differ across regimes. The era boundaries are taken from the descriptive
technology-history literature and are imposed rather than estimated from the data; we
acknowledge in Section 7 that this is an exploratory choice and that formal structural-
break tests would be a natural confirmatory extension. The primary focus is on the “New
Energy Hypothesis,” which posits that in the current AI era, traditional IT investment
indicators decouple from GDP, while energy-related metrics (specifically data center power
consumption) emerge as candidate leading indicators.
3.2 Statistical Techniques
The following statistical methods are applied:
• Rolling Correlations: a 20-quarter (5-year) window is used to track the dynamic
evolution of IT-GDP synchronicity. The window length matches the standard 5-
year Kitchin horizon but interacts uncomfortably with our short AI Era: the AI
Era contains 20 quarterly observations, so within this window the rolling correlation
collapses to a single end-point value once the window slides into the era, carrying
roughly 3-4 “effectively new” end-points after 2021Q1, which we flag explicitly to
discourage reading the trend as a continuous, well-identified estimate over the AI
Era.
4

=== page 5 ===
• Cross-Correlation Analysis (Lead-Lag): we test for lead-lag relationships by
shifting variables between −4 and +4 quarters, in order to characterise whether tech-
nological variables behave as leading, lagging, or coincident indicators.
• Log-Return Transformation: all time-series are converted to quarterly log-returns
(ln(xt/xt−1)) to focus on growth rates and reduce the influence of low-frequency
trends. We run Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) and KPSS tests on every series
in both log-levels and log-returns (full results in stationarity_tests.csv and the
Supplementary Materials Section S5). All eight log-level series are non-stationary;
after log-differencing, ADF rejects the unit-root null at 5% for every series, but the
U.S. semiconductor production and import indices retain a KPSS rejection at p = 0.01
even on log-returns. We flag both as potential residual unit roots and discuss the
implication for headline correlations involving semiconductor variables in Section 7.
• Multiple Testing Correction: we compute Pearson correlations across nine cross-
correlation lags, six counterpart variables (of which three – US IT investment, US
electricity PPI, US semiconductor production – are reported as headline series in
Table 1; the remaining three, US semiconductor imports, US renewables-generation
proxy, and Irish data-center metered electricity, enter the cross-lag, Granger and
spectral panels), and multiple era windows, yielding ∼ 120 tests in total. To control
the false discovery rate at q = 0.05 we apply Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment across
the pooled p-values. Tables 1 and 2 therefore report both raw and FDR-adjusted
significance.
• Granger Causality: where sample sizes allow, we perform Granger (1969) causality
tests to evaluate whether electricity growth predicts future IT investment and GDP
fluctuations. Given the small effective sample for the post-2021 window (N = 20),
Granger results are reported as descriptive rather than as definitive evidence of cau-
sation.
• Frequency-Domain Analysis (Welch PSD): to test whether the canonical 3-
5 year Kitchin periodicity is present in the headline log-return series, we compute
the Welch power spectral density via scipy.signal.welch with sampling frequency
fs = 4 cycles per year (quarterly data), a Hann window, segment length nperseg =
min(32, N ), 50% segment overlap, and constant detrending. The segment length is
chosen short enough that even the Irish data-center series (N = 39 log-return obser-
vations) yields multiple non-overlapping segments, at the cost of a coarser frequency
grid relative to the U.S. series (N ≈ 123). We then report the period (in years) of
the top three non-zero PSD peaks for each series in Section 5.9; the resulting peaks
are interpreted against the canonical Kitchin band of 3-5 years (frequencies 0.20-0.33
cycles per year). PSD estimates are point estimates only; we do not attach formal
confidence bands, since the residual autocorrelation in quarterly log-returns and the
short sample make HAC-corrected spectral inference an explicit caveat (Section 7).

We complement the CCF with Granger causality tests (ssr ftest) over a maximum
lag of four quarters. We restrict the maximum lag from the eight quarters used in earlier
drafts for two reasons: first, a year is the relevant horizon over which compute capacity
might plausibly lead GDP given typical data-center build timelines; second, with only
N ≈ 20 post-COVID quarterly observations, a maximum lag of eight leaves too few residual
degrees of freedom for the auxiliary regressions. Because we select the best lag from the
set {1, 2, 3, 4}, we report both the minimum lag-wise p-value and a Bonferroni-adjusted

is bidirectional between Irish data-center electricity consumption and Irish GDP, since the
CCF profile in Table 2 is mixed about the leading direction.

4.1 Variable Definitions
We work with two parallel sets of series, paired with the GDP aggregate of the economy
in which they are observed.
• U.S. GDP (GDP US): Real Gross Domestic Product, chain-weighted, seasonally
adjusted; FRED series GDPC1. This is the macroeconomic counterpart for all U.S.
variables (IT investment, electricity PPI, semiconductor production and import series,
and the U.S. renewables-generation proxy). Of these, three are reported as headline
series in Table 1; semiconductor imports and the renewables proxy enter the cross-lag,
Granger and spectral panels but not the headline contemporaneous correlations.
• Irish GDP (GDP IE): Real Gross Domestic Product at Constant Market Prices,
seasonally adjusted; Central Statistics Office of Ireland matrix NAQ03, statistic
NAQ03S04. This is the macroeconomic counterpart for the Irish data-center elec-
tricity series reported in Table 2. We discuss in Section 7 the well-known limita-
tions of Irish headline GDP (Modified Gross National Income, GNI*, is the preferred
domestic-activity aggregate) and re-running the same analysis on GNI* is on our
agenda for confirmatory work.
• IT Investment (IT US): U.S. private fixed investment in information processing
equipment, FRED series B935RC1Q027SBEA.
• Electricity PPI (Elec US): U.S. Producer Price Index for industrial electric power
(FRED: WPS054321). This is a price index; it is not the physical quantity of electric-
ity consumed and is therefore not directly comparable to the metered consumption
series used for Ireland (DC IE).
• Semiconductor Metrics (Semi US): Two U.S. series, the import price index for
semiconductors (FRED: IR21320, “Semi Import”) and a production/capacity proxy
(FRED: CAPG3344S, “Semi Production”), used as alternative measures of digital-
inventory accumulation.
• Renewables-generation proxy (PPA Proxy US): FRED series IPN221114T8S,
industrial production index for electric power generation from renewable sources
(utility-scale, U.S., monthly, resampled to quarterly means). We use this as a coarse
auxiliary proxy for U.S. Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) capacity additions, avail-
able only from 2000Q1. It enters the cross-lag, Granger and spectral panels but is
not part of the headline contemporaneous correlations in Table 1; the proxy is ac-
knowledged to be an imperfect substitute for actual contracted PPA volumes, which
are not published as a national quarterly series.
• Data Center Load (DC IE): Metered electricity consumption (gigawatt-hours per
quarter) for the “Data centres” sector in Ireland, CSO matrix MEC02. This is a
physical consumption series.
The asymmetry between “Elec US” (a price index) and “DC IE” (a physical consumption
series) is deliberate: the U.S. side lacks a publicly available quarterly metered series at com-
parable sectoral resolution, while CSO Ireland publishes data-center metered consumption
directly. We flag the asymmetry explicitly so that Tables 1 and 2 are not read as variations
of a single “electricity vs. GDP” relationship.

available of the energy-data nexus, although it now spans only 40 quarterly observations
and ends one year earlier than the headline Irish national-accounts series. Throughout
this subsection, Irish data-center metered electricity consumption (CSO matrix MEC02,

it conflates Ireland-hosted compute capacity with the U.S. macroeconomy and does not
test the within-country lead-lag relationship implied by the “New Energy and Data Cycle”
hypothesis.

5.5 Structural Breakpoint Tests
The era boundaries used in Sections 5.1-5.2 are taken from technology history and are
imposed rather than estimated. To check whether they correspond to genuine empirical
breaks, we run Quandt-Andrews sup-Wald tests for an unknown breakpoint in the con-
temporaneous correlation between each headline pair, with trimming proportion π0 = 0.15.
Andrews (1993) reports an asymptotic 5% critical value of ≈ 8.85 for one parameter and
15% trim, which we use as the rejection threshold; a parametric bootstrap p-value would
tighten this assessment but is not implemented in the present specification.
Three findings emerge. First, the US IT-GDP correlation shows a statistically significant
break in mid-2014 (sup-W = 9.69, above the 5% threshold), with the correlation falling
from +0.45 pre-break to −0.12 post-break. The break date is several years before the start
of our nominal AI Era window (2021Q1), implying that the “Strategic Decoupling” of IT
investment from US GDP is a longer-running phenomenon than the AI-era reading would
suggest, and one that is at most partly attributable to the AI investment cycle. Second,
the US semiconductor production-GDP correlation has the strongest break of the three
pairs (sup-W = 12.66), dated to mid-2020, which is well above the 5% threshold and aligns
naturally with COVID-era supply shocks and the early stages of the post-pandemic “silicon
cycle.” Third, the Irish data-center electricity-GDP correlation has its sup-W maximiser at
2021Q1, which coincides exactly with our nominal AI Era boundary, but the test statistic
itself (2.68) falls well below the critical value: with only 39 paired observations, we cannot
reject the hypothesis of a constant Irish correlation throughout 2015-2024. We therefore
continue to treat the Irish results as exploratory; the boundary at 2021Q1 is, on the available
data, a plausible but unverified marker rather than an empirically established break.

5.7 Robustness: Choice of Irish Activity Measure
The headline AI-Era result “Irish data-center electricity correlates synchronously with Irish
GDP at r = +0.41” depends on which Irish activity aggregate we use as the right-hand-
side variable. Irish real GDP is well known to be inflated by intra-firm activity of large
multinationals (the so-called “leprechaun economics” problem); real GNP, by contrast,
deducts net factor income remitted to the rest of the world and is closer to a domestic-
activity measure. We therefore re-run the synchronous AI-Era correlation against three
alternative activity aggregates available in CSO matrix NAQ03 (Table 5).
The substantive finding is that the headline correlation is not robust to the choice of
activity aggregate. Pairing data-center electricity with Real GDP gives r = +0.41; pairing
the same series with Real GVA gives a very similar r = +0.43; but pairing it with Real

GNP collapses the correlation to r ≈ 0. Since GNP differs from GDP and GVA mainly
by deducting net factor income paid to non-residents, which in Ireland is dominated by
repatriated profits of large multinationals headquartered abroad, the natural interpretation
is that the AI-Era correlation between Irish data-center load and Irish GDP/GVA is driven
by the multinational footprint shared by both series (hyperscaler operations are simultane-

tax structures, and not headline Irish GDP.

6 Discussion: Implications for Macroeconomic Mea-
surement and Forecasting
If a “Digital-Energy Kitchin Cycle” is operative, it has implications for how macroeconomic
forecasters and policy analysts should select and weight leading indicators in data-intensive
economies. We outline the implications below as conjectures consistent with our exploratory
findings; each requires confirmatory work before being treated as a basis for portfolio or
policy decisions.
6.1 Energy as a candidate coincident indicator
On Irish data, the leading-side cross-correlation at k = +4 between Irish data-center elec-
tricity consumption and Irish GDP is negative (r = −0.20), while the largest positive
coefficient is at k = −4, where GDP precedes data-center load. The defensible reading is
that data-center metered consumption in Ireland is a candidate coincident or moderately
lagging indicator of macroeconomic activity, with the strongest signal in the synchronous
AI Era window (r = +0.41, N = 16). Utility-scale load forecasts and Power Purchase
Agreement (PPA) volumes in technology hubs (e.g., Ireland, the U.S. Mid-Atlantic data-
center cluster) remain plausible inputs to nowcasting frameworks such as that of Aruoba
et al. (2009), but our results do not establish a forecasting role for these series, and any such
claim should be tested directly on longer monthly samples with multiple-testing controls.
17

=== page 18 ===
6.2 Apparent decoupling of the silicon cycle
The negative contemporaneous correlation between semiconductor production and GDP
in the AI Era (r = −0.42), reinforced by a significant Quandt-Andrews break in mid-2020
(Table 3) and by a spectral peak at ≈ 4 years (Table 6), is consistent with the “Silicon
Cycle” increasingly behaving as a strategic, longer-term capital-formation series rather
than as a short-cycle inventory series. In the language of Schumpeter (1939), the silicon
cycle appears to be migrating from the Kitchin (short, inventory-driven) wave toward the
Juglar (medium, capital-equipment-driven) wave. If confirmed in larger samples, this would
imply that semiconductor capacity should be treated more in the spirit of Dixit and Pindyck
(1994)-style real-options investment than as a Kitchin-cycle inventory adjustment.
7 Limitations
The empirical results presented in this paper should be read as exploratory rather than
confirmatory. Several limitations qualify their interpretation.
Sample size. The post-2021 “Strategic Decoupling” window contains N = 20 quarterly
observations (df = 18, two-sided 5% critical |r| ≈ 0.44). None of the headline AI-Era
coefficients (r = −0.25 for IT-GDP, r = −0.19 for k = +4 in Table 2, r = −0.42 for
Semi production) reach this threshold individually, although several are jointly suggestive.
Replication on longer monthly samples and additional economies is required before any
pattern can be regarded as established.
Irish GDP measurement. Irish GDP is well known to be heavily influenced by intra-
firm activity of large multinationals (FitzGerald, 2018; Lane, 2017; Central Statistics Office
Ireland, 2017), including intellectual-property relocations that produced an officially re-
ported real GDP growth of 26% in 2015. The preferred domestic-activity aggregate, GNI*,
is only available annually and cannot serve as a quarterly counterpart here. Section 5.7
shows that pairing Irish data-center electricity with quarterly Real GNP (conceptually
closer to GNI*) collapses the AI-Era headline correlation from r = +0.41 to r ≈ 0. Con-
firmatory replication on GNI*, modified domestic demand, or a panel of data-center hubs
less dominated by multinational tax structures remains the natural next step.
Era boundaries are imposed, not estimated. Our six technological eras are taken
from the descriptive technology-history literature. The Quandt-Andrews tests in Sec-
tion 5.5 identify empirically significant breaks at 2014Q3 (US IT-GDP) and 2020Q3 (US
semi-GDP) but cannot reject constant correlation on the smaller Irish sample, and Sec-
tion 5.6, panel (b), shows that Fisher r-to-z at these empirical breaks yields significant de-
coupling for both U.S. pairs after Bonferroni adjustment within the empirical-break family,
while the panel (a) imposed-boundary tests do not. Cloud Expansion therefore conflates
a pre-decoupling sub-regime (1995Q1-2014Q2) with an early post-decoupling sub-regime
(2014Q3-2019Q4), and the headline Cloud-era r = +0.33 should be read as a weighted av-
erage rather than as a stable-regime estimate. A future Bai-Perron multiple-break analysis
on the rolling correlations would offer a still more disciplined characterisation; panel (b) of

Inference assumptions. The Fisher z-transform confidence intervals in Tables 2 and 5
assume i.i.d. bivariate-normal observations; mild residual autocorrelation in quarterly log-
returns makes them approximate. Section 5.3 reports Newey-West HAC standard errors for

Stationarity and spurious correlation. ADF and KPSS tests on every series in log-

semiconductor variable should be discounted accordingly, and a replication on physical-

Causality. Our cross-correlation results are descriptive. Bidirectional Granger tests be-

Bonferroni over the lag set) do not pass 5% after multiplicity adjustment, and the AI-Era-
only restriction leaves the headline test under-powered. The current sample is too short
to disentangle direction of causation; future work should use longer monthly series, panel
methods across multiple data-center hubs, and IV strategies exploiting exogenous variation
in electricity supply (e.g., the EirGrid Dublin grid-connection moratorium, PPA pricing
shocks).
Confounding by COVID-19. The 2021-2025 AI Era window overlaps with persistent
pandemic-related disruptions: 2021Q1-Q3 (reopening volatility), 2021Q4 (Omicron), and
2022Q1 (Omicron tail plus Russia-Ukraine energy shock) are the most directly affected
quarters. Disentangling pandemic effects from a structural “AI arms race” shift would
require explicit pandemic dummies, counterfactual decomposition, or pre-/post-pandemic
instrument strategies, none of which is attempted here.
8 Conclusion
This paper has revisited the Kitchin cycle in light of the digital and AI-driven transfor-
mation of the global economy. Drawing on quarterly U.S. (FRED) and Irish (CSO) data,
we documented three patterns consistent with a recomposition of the cycle around energy
and compute. First, the U.S. contemporaneous IT-GDP correlation shifts from positive in
earlier eras (r = +0.33 during Cloud Expansion) to negative in the AI Era (r = −0.25,
N = 20), with a Quandt-Andrews break at 2014Q3 and a Fisher r-to-z test on the empir-
ical break that survives Bonferroni (z = +3.11, pBonf = 0.006); the analogous Cloud-vs-AI

imposed-boundary test does not (pBonf = 0.164), so “Strategic Decoupling” is a longer-
running, pre-AI phenomenon. Second, the Irish AI-Era synchronous correlation between
data-centre electricity and Real GDP (r = +0.41, N = 16) collapses to r ≈ 0 when Real
GDP is replaced by Real GNP, indicating that the headline is largely an artefact of a shared
multinational tax-structure footprint; the cross-correlation profile is also asymmetric on the
“GDP precedes data-centre load” side (k = −4, r = +0.28, vs. k = +4, r = −0.19), con-
sistent with compute capacity as a coincident or moderately lagging indicator. Third, U.S.
semiconductor production shows a progressively negative GDP correlation across post-1991
eras (r = −0.13 → −0.42), a Quandt-Andrews break at 2020Q3, and a spectral peak at 4
years inside the Kitchin band, while neither U.S. nor Irish GDP shows a comparable peak.
The evidence remains exploratory: only two of ∼ 120 correlation tests cross an un-
corrected 5% threshold, none survive Benjamini-Hochberg FDR adjustment, and the era
boundaries are partly empirical (U.S.) and partly imposed (Irish). Three confirmatory ex-
tensions are valuable: monthly-frequency replication across data-centre hubs (Ireland, the
Netherlands, Singapore, Northern Virginia, Frankfurt); re-estimating Irish results against
GNI* or a multinational-adjusted GDP series; and identification strategies exploiting ex-
ogenous variation in grid capacity and PPA pricing (e.g., the EirGrid Dublin connection
moratorium). If these patterns survive, the Kitchin cycle of the next decade may be best
monitored not by warehouse stock-to-sales ratios but by dashboards of contracted electricity
and compute; if not, the more conservative conclusion is that the cycle has dematerialised
faster than the data infrastructure required to measure it.
