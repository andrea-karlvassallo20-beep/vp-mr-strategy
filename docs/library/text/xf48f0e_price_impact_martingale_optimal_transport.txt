Price-impact Martingale optimal transport
Erhan Bayraktar∗ Shuoqing Deng† Daxin Huang‡ Dominykas Norgilas§
Abstract
Martingale optimal transport (MOT) provides model-independent bounds for option prices
calibrated to vanilla options, assuming frictionless trading in the underlying. In this work, we
consider the case where dynamic trading incurs quadratic temporary price impact. First, we
prove a duality result for the superhedging price. In particular, the primal of the duality
relaxes the strict martingale constraint by penalizing the deviation from a specific martingale.
Second, for one asset and one trading period, we interpret this price-impact martingale optimal
transport (PIMOT) as a weak optimal transport (WOT) problem, and provide a characteriza-
tion in the spirit of Wasserstein projection. Finally, we justify that PIMOT is an interpolation
between MOT and classical optimal transport (OT), and derive the convergence rate to both
sides. Numerical examples with SPX option data show how the price bounds per claim depend
on the order size.
Mathematics Subject Classification (2020): 91G20, 60G05, 49M29.
Keywords: martingale optimal transport, price impact, weak optimal transport, robust hedg-

Classical MOT treats both parts of the hedge as frictionless. Liang et al. [25] introduce bid–
ask MOT, which brings the bid–ask spreads of vanilla options into this framework. They show
that the resulting value is the superhedging price when options are bought at the ask and sold
at the bid, and they quantify its convergence to MOT as the spreads vanish. Frictions can also
enter through the dynamic part of the hedge, as costs of trading the underlying. Dolinsky and
Soner [14] charge proportional costs to both the options and the stock, and their dual problem
imposes approximate marginal and martingale conditions. Cheridito, Kupper and Tangpi [12]
allow convex costs for dynamic trading and keep the marginal constraints of MOT when call bid
and ask prices coincide.
The two parts of a semi-static hedge are executed differently. Consider a dealer who sells a
large exotic claim on an underlying with low trading volume. The static hedge is a single trade
at time zero, of known size and at a price agreed per contract, so the dealer takes the option
prices as given. The dynamic hedge is rebalanced at every fixing date in path-dependent amounts
that must be executed promptly, and temporary impact is the cost of this immediacy. Gu´eant
and Pu [21] point out that such costs cannot be ignored when the nominal of the option is large
compared with the volume traded in the underlying. Their model and that of Bank, Soner and
Voß [6] charge these costs to the trades in the underlying. A spread on the options is paid once
and grows linearly in the quantity, while temporary impact grows quadratically in the size of each
order and recurs at every rebalancing. We therefore keep the quoted call prices for the static part
and charge impact to the dynamic part only.

Our second contribution concerns the one-asset, one-period problem. Proposition 3.1 formu-
lates PIMOT as weak optimal transport [1, 5, 20] with a quadratic penalty on the conditional drift.
Theorem 3.2 shows that, whenever the value is finite, all optimal couplings share the same drift
and the objective gap controls the squared L2(µ0) distance to that drift. For square-integrable
marginals, the theorem also gives an equivalent optimization over intermediate laws in the convex-
order set used in Wasserstein projection [3, 19]. The unique optimal intermediate law is the law
of Eγ [X1 | X0] under any optimal coupling γ.
Finally, when all impact coefficients are multiplied by ε > 0, the PIMOT price decreases to
the MOT value as ε ↓ 0 and increases to the OT value as ε → ∞ (Theorem 4.2). The gaps are of
order 1/ε at the OT end and, under a bounded frictionless superhedge, of order ε at the MOT end
(Propositions 4.4 and 4.5). Since n claims at impact κ have the same price per unit as one claim
at impact nκ, the price interval per claim widens from the MOT interval towards the OT interval
as the order grows. Section 5 illustrates this with forward-start and basket calls, including SPX
data.
Organization of the paper. Section 2 introduces the market with quadratic temporary
impact, proves the duality and characterizes the absence of arbitrage across maturities. Section 3
treats one asset and one trading period, derives the weak transport formulation and describes
the optimal couplings. Section 4 derives the OT and MOT limits and their convergence rates.
Section 5 computes price bounds for the forward-start and basket calls. Section 6 contains the
proofs, and Appendix A describes the calibration and the numerical implementation.

2 Model and Duality
We consider a semi-static market in which vanilla options can be traded at exogenous quotes,
while dynamic trading in the underlying incurs temporary price impact. All prices are expressed
in units of cash, with zero interest and no dividends. The static part of the hedging strategy is
set up once, while the dynamic part is rebalanced at every fixed date in amounts that depend on
the realized path. As motivated in the Introduction, we charge execution costs to the dynamic
part only. Section 2.1 describes the market, Section 2.2 formulates the superhedging problem and
its primal counterpart, and Section 2.3 states the duality; Section 2.4 treats no-arbitrage.
2.1 The market
As in [15, Section 2], calls are quoted on individual assets, and a position in any one asset may
depend on the observed history of the entire price vector. In our market, the temporary impact
on each asset’s execution price depends only on the size of the order in that asset.

Remark 2.1 (Calibration across assets). At each quoted maturity i, calls on asset a at all strikes
determine the law µa i of X a i , as in [15, Section 2] and [7, Section 2]. The main formulation in
[2, Section 4] and the model in [22, Section 1.1] fix the joint law of Xi at each calibration date.
The alternative in [2, Remark 4.3] fixes only the individual marginal laws. For d ≥ 2, the joint
law determines the expectation of the basket call (X 1 i + X 2 i − K)+. In our market this expectation
can vary over Π(µ) with the dependence between the two assets.
