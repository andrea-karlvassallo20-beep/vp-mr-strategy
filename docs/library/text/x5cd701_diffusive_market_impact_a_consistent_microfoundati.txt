Diffusive Market Impact: A Consistent Microfoundation
Julius F. Bonart
October 14, 2026
Abstract
Structural price diffusivity explains many empirical regularities of market impact
including the “square-root law” and its crossover to a linear regime for low trading rates
[Bonart, 2026a]. One of its central predictions is that an information-neutral trading
strategy generates a diffusive impact state. We microfound this result in an economy
with a trader and many arbitrageurs who progressively eliminate predictable returns.
Each arbitrageur observes realized returns and a private signal of one component of the
fundamental return. As aggregate private information becomes complete and under
suitable convergence to a stationary limit, the trader’s impact law is of the form j =
U ·w, where w is the innovation in the trading schedule and U is causal all-pass. Impact
returns are therefore white, even though no individual arbitrageur can reconstruct the
underlying trading strategy. We then investigate the economic meaning of the impact
phase. We argue that the U which has minimum distance from the filter L generating
the trade flow is an especially interesting candidate: Trade flow is then minimally
distorted under impact and arbitrage, and it always guarantees positive impact costs.
Under a trader flow generated by a biexponential L (the simplest form allowed in our
model) an interesting result emerges: Impact decline is confined to a narrow band
of 50%–60%, lower than some empirical estimates and quite consistent with others.
More complicated flow models can lead to different decays. Finally, we argue that
in real markets, the impact phase is probably somewhat “distorted” in its long-term
tails which allows for a full relaxation of the impact propagator. The effect is weak,
long-term mean-reversion of the impact state.
(1) Introduction
The causal response of prices to trades – market impact – is central to price formation and
to the economics of trading. It determines how order flow enters prices and, from a practical
perspective, is tantamount to a trading cost [Bouchaud, 2009]. Market impact therefore
limits the capacity of investment strategies [Bonelli et al., 2019] and is a central input to
execution, portfolio construction, and backtesting.
A common empirical workflow is to measure the price response to trades, control for
informational alpha, infer a primitive impact kernel, and use that kernel to predict impact

under alternative trading scenarios. Two assumptions underlie this procedure.
The first concerns causality. An impulse response estimated from observational data is
identified with the causal effect of trades on prices. But order flow is endogenous: trades
affect returns, while expected returns and market conditions also affect trades. This is the
familiar problem of causal inference [Pearl, 2009], and practitioners are well aware of it
[Webster, 2023].
The second concerns extrapolation. An impact law estimated under one distribution of
order flow is assumed to remain valid when that distribution changes. Optimal execution
models, for example, typically optimize over a family of execution schedules while holding
the impact model fixed [Almgren and Chriss, 2001]. This assumes that the market response
itself does not adapt materially to the new trading regime.
In Bonart [2026a], I have proposed a framework that rejects this invariance assumption.
The starting point is simple: prices are structurally diffusive. This means that in mature
and liquid markets, persistent return predictability attracts arbitrage and is progressively
eliminated. A sufficiently persistent change in trading behavior should therefore trigger an
adaptive market response. Following this argument, it turns out that the impact law of a
trader must depend on the statistics of that trader’s order flow [Bonart, 2026a].
The argument rests on the following thought experiment. Consider a trader running a
long-short strategy. Compare two worlds: one in which the trader participates, and one
in which she does not. The difference between the realized and counterfactual price is the
trader’s market impact. But if prices remain diffusive in both worlds, their difference cannot
be arbitrary. Structural diffusivity imposes a covariance constraint on impact.
This constraint becomes especially strong when the trader’s flow is information-neutral,
meaning that it is unrelated to fundamental price innovations. This benchmark is relevant for
trading driven by rebalancing, hedging, or long-horizon allocation rather than short-horizon
return forecasts; see, for example, Bucci et al. [2019]. In this case, Bonart [2026a] shows that
impact returns must be white. Equivalently, the cumulative impact state is diffusive.
Specifically in the linear causal setting of Bonart [2026a], the whitening constraint takes a
particularly simple form. Let jt denote the impact return and wt the innovation in executed
volume. Then
jt ∝ (U · w)t , (1.1)
where · denotes convolution and U is a causal all-pass filter. By construction,
E[jtjt+h] ∝ δ[h] .
Under weak assumptions, this restriction then also determines how impact scales with
time and traded volume. The empirical phenomenology of market impact, including the
square-root law of metaorder impact and its crossover to a linear regime for low trading
rates, is largely recovered.
Square-root impact need therefore not reflect an anomalous response mechanism [Bouchaud
et al., 2006]. It can arise as a by-product of diffusivity. This picture also changes the inter-
pretation of optimal execution and price manipulation arguments. A fixed impact law may
admit a cost-minimizing or apparently price-manipulating strategy, while that strategy may

itself move the market outside the regime over which the impact law remains invariant. The
opportunity and the model that generates it can therefore be mutually inconsistent. The
impact law becomes a moving target.
Main contributions. Two questions were left open. First, why should decentralized trad-
ing generate the fixed point Eq. (1.1)? The result is especially puzzling because the impact
law adapts to the statistics of an individual strategy even though no market participant
need observe that strategy. A microfoundation should explain how such participant-specific
adaptation can emerge without participant-specific identification.
Second, the special case U = 1 is a pure surprise model: impact is proportional to the
innovation in order flow. But this case implies zero expected impact costs. Positive costs
therefore require a nontrivial phase. This begs the question whether the all-pass freedom al-
ways contains economically admissible phases? Bonart [2026a] established positive expected
costs for a restricted class of order-flow processes and left the general existence result open.
This paper addresses both questions.
I consider an economy with one trader and many arbitrageurs. Realized returns are
public. Each arbitrageur also observes a private signal correlated with one component of the
counterfactual return. No arbitrageur has enough information to reconstruct the trader’s
strategy. Collectively, however, their information becomes complete when every fundamental
component is covered.
Each arbitrageur uses the information available to them to eliminate a predictable com-
ponent of returns. Sequentially, these interventions remove more and more forecastable
structure. When the stationary limit exists, and under complete aggregate information,
residual impact converges to the structural form Eq. (1.1). The fixed point predicted by
structural diffusivity therefore emerges from decentralized arbitrage even though no individ-
ual arbitrageur observes the underlying strategy.
The phase U has a natural economic interpretation. Part of it may be inherited from
primitive impact. Part may be generated by the way prices adjust to arbitrage – for example
through delay or smoothing. Diffusivity removes predictable variation but does not uniquely
determine the timing of that adjustment.
Finally, I return to impact costs and establish some new results. For trading schedules
generated as differences of stationary inventory processes, I show that for arbitrary sta-
tionary flow statistics there exist nontrivial causal all-passes that generate strictly positive
expected impact costs. Specifically, I discuss an economically plausible “minimum distortion
criterion” under which an exogenous trader’s flow is minimally perturbed by arbitrage. This
is equivalent to seeking an all-pass U ∗ that maximizes overlap with the flow filter L. I then
show that this overlap-maximizing U ∗ also generates positive impact costs. Moreover, when
L is a biexponential kernel – this is the simplest benchmark admitted in my framework –
impact decay generated by the corresponding U ∗ is confined to a narrow band of 50%–60%
of peak impact. This is result is interesting as it shows that plausible economic selection
criteria can steer the all-pass freedom towards quite realistic impact dynamics.

(2) Relevant literature
Empirical evidence of participant-level heterogeneity in impact functions is provided by
Bladon et al. [2012]. Quite interestingly, direct evidence supporting impact invariance is
also limited: To the best of our knowledge, only T´oth et al. [2018] compare propagators
inferred from an asset manager’s trades with those fitted on the rest of the market and find
no significant differences. However, the order-flow statistics of the asset manager is close to
that of the rest of the market. Within the diffusive impact framework of Bonart [2026a], this
similarity limits our ability to distinguish between universal propagator and a participant-
specific one. A more discriminating empirical test would compare a strategy with a genuinely
different autocorrelation than the one observed in the wider market flow. To our knowledge,
such a direct test is still outstanding.
A non-universal impact law does not imply that the measured impact of metaorders –
single investment decisions executed incrementally – must also be non-universal. In partic-
ular, a universal scaling law for measured impact, such as the square-root law [Sato and
Kanazawa, 2025, Maitrier et al., 2026], can be compatible with participant-specific impact
laws. In the framework of Bonart [2026a], such laws whiten individual order flow and can
generate square-root metaorder impact under the relevant assumptions on order-flow statis-
tics.
Since Kyle’s seminal paper [Kyle, 1985], impact models fall under two broad categories:
mechanistic and information-driven. In mechanistic models impact is encoded via mechanical
changes of demand and supply curves under trading pressure [Donier et al., 2014]. The mech-
anistic interpretation emphasizes that trading is generally anonymous in modern markets;
hence the collective market cannot distinguish different participants and impact is therefore
represented by a universal, participant-agnostic response mechanism. Information-based ap-
proaches emphasize what prices reveal about individual trading. Square-root impact can
then emerges under residual uncertainty from efficiency and fair-pricing conditions [Farmer
et al., 2013], Bayesian learning [Saddier and Marsili, 2024], or as a consequence of structural
price diffusivity as in our framework. Here, arbitrageurs exploit the perturbations created
by individual participants through their primitive, mechanical impact. Thus, Bonart [2026a]
takes mechanical impact as given but challenges that its potentially universal primitive shape
remains intact under influences of arbitrage traders.
The setup of our model draws inspiration from Hellwig [1980] and its notion of dispersed
information across agents that are visible through prices. Our iterative arbitrage process
is also related to He and Wang [1995]’s paradigm of iterative generation of trading volume
under private signals. More recent closely related references include Barato et al. [2013] who
study the statistical arbitrage opportunities created by an external flow in a Minority Game
in which participants continuously adapt their strategies. Muhle-Karbe et al. [2026] derive

(3) Review of the existing framework
Bonart [2026a] derives restrictions on market impact from a single structural requirement:
prices are diffusive both with and without the trader. The argument does not ask how
primitive impact is generated. It asks instead which impact processes are compatible with
arbitraged prices, and it finds that the answer is strongly constrained.

Bonart [2026a]. Consider a trader whose impact is defined relative to a counterfactual
market in which she does not trade. The realized return decomposes as
xt = yt + jt , (3.1)

The central assumption is that continuous arbitrage keeps both the realized and the
counterfactual price diffusive, i. e. that if the trader had never traded, prices would still
be diffusive. In other words, diffusivity observed today is structural and not observed by
chance.
Initial reactions to this paradigm may vary – with qualifiers ranging from ”immediately
compelling” to ”strong assumption”. Perhaps, the diffusivity assumption becomes more
transparent under the stationary regime that Bonart [2026a] focuses on: Markets do not
have to quickly restore diffusivity when a trader enters or exits the market. In fact, the
framework does not apply to a one-shot intervention, or in the case where a trading strategy
enters a hitherto unexplored trading envelope that creates a structural break with its past.
What is assumed is that the “equilibration time” needed for arbitrage to become effec-
tive is short enough so that impact measurements on typical observational datasets operate
de facto under the stationary regime. In other words: When establishing the square-root
law empirically, a long (typically years) order flow history must be analyzed, and it may
be reasonable to assume that markets restore diffusivity after perturbations are detected
persistently over such long horizons, even if the process is not fast.
Under the stationary regime of trading strategies that persistently perturb the return
autocorrelation, what matters for arbitrage is then not so much the signal-to-noise ratio over
finite trading episodes: Any departure from price diffusivity can in principle be detected on
sufficiently long horizons. A detectability criterion based on the signal-to-noise ratio (see
Bouchaud et al. [2026]) is therefore not very meaningful, unless a minimum payoff expected
by an arbitrageur is specified, typically a fraction of the bid-ask spread. Following this route,
Bonart [2026a] derives a “detectability criterion” which supports the conclusion that even
small participation rates lead to whitened impact over short, intraday horizons.

returns but how: It remains unclear why an optimal arbitrage intervention would lead to
Eq. (3.6).

famous model [Kyle, 1985] can be regarded as a Kalman filter on combined order flow.
Interpreting the market maker’s quoting rule as the trader’s causal impact, the resulting
impact depends on aggregate rather than individual flow. The implication is peculiar: not
trading for a finite period may generate “impact” in Kyle’s model. To see this, define
the counterfactual as the world where the trader permanently exits the market. This would
change the information conveyed by order flow and, consequently, the market maker’s pricing
rule. Hence, additional impact – growing differences between actual and counterfactual
prices – can be generated even without direct activity from the trader’s strategy. Such a
multi-channel impact mechanism represents a significant departure from [Bonart, 2026a] and
indeed many other impact models (e.g. propagator and latent liquidity models).
Aggregate informational completeness. We conclude that a consistent microfounda-
tion of Bonart [2026a] cannot solely rely on the mechanism above. The solution is to consider
an exogenous trader in a many-arbitrageur economy, in which return whiteness emerges from
repeated arbitrage interventions by agents who individually hold little information, but are
collectively informed about the counterfactual y. This aggregate “informational complete-
ness” is the central assumption in this paper. In a way that is mathematically rigorously
defined, the collective arbitrageur sector “observes” the counterfactual y, although each in-
dividual arbitrageur accesses only little private information. The individual information
channels may even asymptotically vanish when the number of arbitrageurs grows.
Aggregate informational completeness may be a realistic description of modern markets:
price is a deterministic function of the collective actions of all participants. Hence, the com-
ponents of y may be exactly “known”, collectively, by the counterfactual market (excluding
the external trader). In this sense, informational completeness is tantamount to an arbitrage
sector which lies “dense” in the market.

(4) A many-arbitrageur economy
We now present a suitable microfoundation. The economy contains one trader, a collection
of utility maximizing arbitrageurs, and a common counterparty. All trades with the counter-
party generate mechanical price impact. The trader’s flow q against the counterparty is taken
as exogenous whereas the arbitrage trades are determined endogenously through maximiz-
ing arbitrageurs’ internal utility. Absent arbitrage, returns x are the sum of a fundamental
return y and the mechanical impact of the trader’s flow.
The fundamental return is itself composed of independent components y(i). Each ar-
bitrageur i observes a private signal of component i, whereas no arbitrageur observes the
primitive innovation w driving trader flow. Arbitrageurs are rational in the sense that, when-
ever they act, they use all information available in their own filtration to form the optimal
linear forecast of the next returns and trade against their predictable component. Their
trading policies are therefore linear and strictly causal: today’s intervention can depend on
past prices and past private signals, but not on contemporaneous innovations.

(5) Adaptation in the many-arbitrageur economy
In this technical section we rigorously establish the existence of convergent subsequences of
the arbitrage process in our economy. Each such cluster point is an arbitraged market in
which returns are white and uncorrelated from past components of the counterfactual price.
Under informational completeness – in which the histories of the counterfactual price com-
ponents y(i) are known to the collective arbitrage sector – impact satisfies an orthogonality

(9) Discussion
Informational completeness is a substantial assumption in this paper. The essential role
played by private information in price formation is recognized in the economics literature,
but it remains almost anathema to the econophysics school of finance. Let us therefore
critically examine the assumption.
Aggregate informational completeness does not imply that any individual arbitrageur is
well informed, but rather that y is “known” collectively. Consider sophisticated arbitrageurs
who distinguish their interventions a(i), aimed at eliminating return predictability associated
with order-flow autocorrelations, from trades b(i) driven by other, independent signals, possi-
bly including fundamental ones. Assume further that the collective arbitrage sector drives y
through its aggregate trades P i b(i). From this we cannot yet conclude that y is collectively
observed.
The external trader may incidentally distort b(i). Symmetric liquidity effects that remain
orthogonal to future returns complicate the analysis but do not alter the covariance argument
which leads to white impact returns. A plausible directional distortion may arise if liquidity is
distorted asymmetrically by jt. In this case, asymmetric changes in the execution conditions
of b(i), mediated by jt, may create correlations between b(i) and j. However, this is plausibly
a second-order effect in j.
More seriously, consider the distortion of a fundamental mispricing by the exogenous
trader’s cumulative impact state, It = P v<t jv. Relative to the counterfactual, a mispricing
is shifted by −δ(i)It for some δ(i). Suppose now that at least one of the arbitrageurs trades

We have studied how decentralized arbitrage can generate diffusive impact without iden-
tifying the underlying trader. On a finite observation window, every accumulation point
of the adjustment process has uncorrelated returns; aggregate informational completeness
additionally whitens the trader’s impact returns. Under the stated stationary-limit assump-
tions, impact therefore takes a causal all-pass form. Its remaining phase freedom determines
trading costs and relaxation. A minimum-distortion criterion yields positive costs and, in
a biexponential benchmark, a narrow range of conditional impact decay. Deriving phase
selection from trading behavior, and testing the predicted dependence on participant-level
order-flow statistics remain the central next steps.
