Why financial prices exhibit Brownian motion despite predictable order flow:
An exactly solvable nonlinear price impact model
Yuki Sato,1 Shunta Fujiwara,2 and Kiyoshi Kanazawa2
1Institute of Systems and Information Engineering, University of Tsukuba, Tennodai, Tsukuba, Ibaraki 305-8573, Japan∗
2Department of Physics, Graduate School of Science, Kyoto University, Kyoto 606-8502, Japan
(Dated: November 6, 2026)
In econophysics, there are two enigmatic empirical laws of financial market microstructure: (i) the market-
order flow has predictable persistence due to metaorder splitting by institutional investors, as formulated by the
Lillo–Mike–Farmer model. However, this phenomenon seems paradoxical given the diffusive and unpredictable
price dynamics. (ii) The price impact I(Qtot) of a large metaorder Qtot follows the square-root law, I(Qtot) ∝√Qtot. In this Letter, we reveal why price dynamics follows Brownian motion despite predictable order flow
by unifying these enigmas via an exactly solvable model. We generalize the Lillo–Mike–Farmer model to
nonlinear price-impact dynamics, which is mapped to a solvable L´evy-walk model. Our exact solution shows
that the price dynamics remains diffusive under the square-root law, even under persistent order flow, reflecting
the finite variance of metaorder impact; our model is thus a minimal representative of the universality class of
nonlinear price-impact models with finite-variance metaorder impact. This work illustrates the crucial role of
the square-root law in mitigating price movements by large metaorders, leading to Brownian price dynamics,
consistent with the efficient market hypothesis over long timescales.
Keywords: econophysics, market microstructure, diffusivity puzzle, the square-root law, the Lillo–Mike–Farmer model, L´evy
walks
Introduction. There are two empirical enigmas in econo-
physics [1–3]. The first enigma is the long memory of the
market-order flow. A market order is a decision to buy or sell
stocks immediately, and its sign is represented by ϵt = +1
(ϵt = −1) for a buy (sell) market order at time t (e.g.,
{ϵt}t≥1 = {+, +, +, −, −, +} signifies three consecutive
buy orders, followed by two sell orders and then one buy or-
der). The long memory implies that such order signs are easily
predictable and, thus, the autocorrelation (ACF) of the market-
order signs decays very slowly:
E[ϵtϵt+τ ] ∝ τ −γ , 0 < γ < 1, (1)
where E[. . . ] denotes an ensemble average over stationary re-
alizations of the order-flow process and the sum of the ACF
diverges as P∞ τ =1 E[ϵtϵt+τ ] = ∞. Empirically, the ACF is
estimated by a time average under the assumptions of station-
arity and ergodicity. This formula implies that the order flow
has persistence for a very long time, such as for a few days [1].
Then, why does this predictability arise ubiquitously in
financial markets? This order-sign predictability originates
from the metaorder splitters and is well formulated by the
Lillo–Mike–Farmer (LMF) model [4, 5]. The LMF theory
argues that institutional investors have hidden large orders
(called metaorders), which are split into a series of child or-
ders. Therefore, large metaorders cause persistent order flow
because all child orders have the same order sign during the
execution of a single metaorder (see the left panel of Fig. 1).
In particular, the LMF theory predicted a relation between the
market microstructure and the ACF,
γ = α − 1, 1 < α < 2, (2)
by assuming a power-law distribution ψm(Qtot) ∝ Q−α−1 tot
for the total metaorder size Qtot. Recently, Sato and
Kanazawa validated this prediction by scrutinizing the micro-
scopic dataset of the Tokyo Stock Exchange (TSE) [6, 7] and,
thereby, have established the LMF theory as the causality the-
ory for the long memory.
However, this phenomenon is very counter-intuitive: given
that the market-order flow is predictable, why is the price dy-
namics unpredictable? This puzzle arises because the price
dynamics would exhibit predictable anomalous diffusion if
the price impact were linear with respect to the metaorder size,
as documented in the linear propagator model [1, 8, 9] (except
under the fine-tuned balance condition between the order-flow
and price-impact decays [10]). Why is the price dynamics
diffusive despite the predictable order flow? This is the first
empirical enigma, known as the price diffusivity puzzle; it is
particularly puzzling because most traditional economic theo-
ries predict linear price-impact models [11].
The second enigma is the nonlinearity of the price impact,
called the square-root law (SRL) [1, 12] (see Fig. 1). In
practice, the linear price-impact model is valid only for small
metaorder size Qtot; for large Qtot, it has been reported that
the price impact is a nonlinear function of the metaorder size
Qtot:
I(Qtot) := E[ϵ∆m | Qtot] ∝ Qδ tot, δ ≈ 1 2 , (3)
where ∆m is the price change caused by a metaorder of total
size Qtot. Recently, Sato and Kanazawa scrutinized the TSE
dataset, provided a very accurate estimation of δ, and estab-
lished the strict universality of the SRL, such that δ is exactly
equal to 1/2 for all liquid stocks on the TSE within statisti-
cal errors [13]. To date, the cause of the strict universality of
the SRL remains unclear. One of the most promising mod-
els might be the latent limit order-book model [1, 12, 14], but
partially negative evidence was observed recently [15]. Thus,

In this Letter, together with the companion paper [16], we
propose a minimal model that naturally extends the LMF
framework to incorporate nonlinear price-impact dynamics,
thereby unifying these two enigmas from a single, coherent
perspective. We consider the system composed of M order
splitters whose metaorder-size statistics follow the power law
ψm(Qtot) ∝ Q−α−1 tot with 1 < α < 2. The price impact
of each metaorder is assumed to obey the nonlinear scaling
I(Qtot) ∝ Qδ tot with 0 < δ ≤ 1. Crucially, this model
is exactly solvable. The price-impact contribution by a sin-
gle trader can be mapped to the L´evy-walk theory [17, 18]
with nonlinear space–time coupling [19]. Our exact solution
shows that the price dynamics is always diffusive even in the
presence of the long memory if δ = 1/2. This result im-
plies that the SRL plays the crucial role in mitigating the price
impact due to the huge liquidity consumption by institutional
investors. Additionally, we find that natural extensions of our
model exhibit behavior consistent with other empirical laws
(such as the inverse-cubic law [1, 3, 20–24] and volatility clus-
tering [2, 3]). Thus, our minimal model is consistent with var-
ious empirical laws in econophysics.
LMF Model. We start from the Lillo–Mike–Farmer
(LMF) model with M statistically independent order splitters
indexed by Ω = {1, 2, . . . , M }. Each splitter repeatedly exe-
cutes signed metaorders of total size Q(i) tot, where Q(i) tot is ran-
domly drawn from the empirically observed power-law total
metaorder-size distribution ψm(Qtot):
ψm(Qtot) ∝ (Qtot)−α−1, 1 < α < 2. (4)
At each time step, one splitter i ∈ Ω is randomly selected with
equal probability λ := 1/M and submits a unit child order.
During a metaorder execution, all child orders share the same
order sign ϵ(i). Once the current metaorder is completed, the
splitter independently draws a new sign ϵ(i) ∈ {−1, +1} with
equal probability and a new total size Q(i) tot. In this Letter, we
extend the original LMF framework by incorporating a price
process driven by nonlinear price impact.
Price dynamics for M = 1. Let us introduce a price pro-
cess into the LMF model. To develop our intuition, we first
describe the single-splitter dynamics and then extend it to the
full M-body system.
For M = 1, let q(1)(t) be the volume already executed im-
mediately before time t within the current metaorder of split-

from the total metaorder size Q(1) tot. The counter q(1)(t) is in-

q(1)(t) is reset to zero, and a new sign and a new total size
Q(1) tot are drawn for the next metaorder. In this case, splitter 1
deterministically submits a child order at every time step.

erated by metaorder splitting (see Fig. 2 for a schematic).

L´evy-walk mapping This model (7) can be regarded as
a discrete-time L´evy-walk model with nonlinear space-time

Short-memory price dynamics under the square-root law.
Furthermore, the SRL suppresses long-range predictability in
price dynamics. Let us consider the continuous-time velocity
v(t) := dm(t)/dt as the counterpart of the discrete-time re-
turn process. In the stationary state, the velocity correlation
asymptotically behaves as
Cr (τ ) := E[v(t)v(t + τ )]ss ∝ τ −θ , θ = α − 2δ + 1. (11)
When the price impact obeys the SRL, δ = 1/2, the decay
exponent becomes θ = α > 1. The price-change correlation
is short-ranged, in sharp contrast to the long-ranged order-sign
ACF with exponent γ = α−1 < 1. Thus, the SRL suppresses
not only anomalous diffusion but also long-range memory in
the price dynamics.
These two results suggest that the market exhibits strong
resilience against large liquidity consumption by order split-
ters thanks to the sufficiently concave nature of the SRL. This
interpretation is crucially important: it is the SRL that sup-
presses the large price movements in the presence of the long
memory. This scenario highlights the importance of study-
ing the microscopic origin of the SRL for stable regulation of
financial markets.
Concluding discussion. We develop an exactly solvable
model of nonlinear price-impact dynamics in the presence of
metaorder splitters, as a minimal representative of the univer-
sality class with finite-variance metaorder impact. Our theory
clarifies the practical importance of the SRL regarding mar-
ket stability. The presence of the long memory implies that
markets suffer from the large liquidity consumption by order
splitters. It has been mysterious why markets operate consis-
tently with the diffusive nature even in the presence of such a
large demand. Our theory shows that the concavity of the SRL
suppresses large price movements caused by order splitters,
which leads to diffusive price dynamics with short memory.
In other words, if the price impact were less concave than the
SRL (δ > 1/2), the price would be superdiffusive with clear
predictability for 1 < α < α∗ := 2δ.
Our theory highlights that the SRL is not a minor technical
topic but a key topic in financial economics, directly related
to the theoretical foundation of the efficient market hypoth-
esis (EMH)—the hypothesis that the price is unpredictable
(thus diffusive) from public information. However, most tra-
ditional economic theories (such as Kyle’s model [11]) pre-
dict the linear price-impact law, which apparently contradicts
the EMH in the presence of the predictable market-order flow.
Our theory clarifies that, even under predictable market-order
flow, the long-timescale price dynamics is consistent with the
EMH thanks to the SRL. There is room to update financial-
economic theories by incorporating the SRL.
Our model also serves as a minimal null model questioning
the previous statistical evidence for fine-tuned impact decay.
Within the linear propagator model, the fine-tuned impact de-
cay G(τ ) ∝ τ −βc , βc := (1 − γ)/2, has been proposed as
a resolution of the diffusivity puzzle [8, 9], and propagators
inferred from data have been reported to be consistent with
it [1]. However, we find that the same propagator estimation
applied to our model returns the fine-tuned decay as a sta-
tistical artifact [25], although our model has no true impact
decay. More generally, for diffusive prices and long-memory
order flow, propagator estimation returns the fine-tuned decay
irrespective of the true impact [25], so that the inferred decay
alone is not evidence for transient impact. Thus, our model
provides a minimal counterexample to the causal reading of
the inferred fine-tuned decay as true impact decay.
As documented in our companion paper [16], the present
framework can naturally accommodate additional empirical

ingredients, such as resting periods between metaorder execu-

not change the central conclusions concerning diffusion and
short memory of this Letter. Furthermore, such extended for-
mulations may help explain broader empirical regularities of
financial prices. For example, models incorporating resting
periods or impact decay exhibit behavior consistent with the

For a more quantitative understanding of financial markets,
it would be natural to incorporate market-microstructure dy-
namics and time-dependent endogenous/exogenous parame-

book dynamics, intraday seasonality, and external shocks.
Such extensions are left as future studies.

In this End Matter, we outline our theoretical calculations
only for the continuous-time model, since the discrete-time
LMF L´evy-walk model and its continuous-time counterpart
exhibit the same asymptotic MSD scaling and short-memory
property.
