ABSTRACT 
This paper decomposes the return sources of a hybrid portfolio combining a fundamentalist screen 
— based on P/E (price-to-earnings), ROE (return on equity), Net Margin, Current Ratio, and P/BV 
(price-to-book value) — with a moving-average crossover (MAC) system using simple moving 
averages (SMA) of 9 and 46 periods, applied to the Brazilian stock exchange (B3) from January 
2000 to December 2025 (300 monthly observations). CAPM and Fama-French three- and four-
factor models estimated by OLS (ordinary least squares) with heteroskedasticity-robust standard 
errors identify a positive and statistically significant Jensen alpha of 1.11% per month (p = 0.005) 
in the three-factor model. In the four-factor model with momentum, the alpha remains significant 
(0.98% p.m.; p = 0.009), while the momentum factor does not reach statistical significance (p = 
0.409). The central — and unexpected — finding is the non-significance of the HML (high-minus-
low) factor (βh = 0.1266; p = 0.544): despite explicit value criteria in the screen, returns do not 
load on the value premium, likely because ROE and Net Margin requirements tilt the selection 
toward quality firms whose portfolios historically exhibit low correlation with pure HML. By 
contrast, the SMB (small-minus-big) factor is highly significant (βs = 0.38; p = 0.000), and the 
portfolio displays a defensive profile (market beta ≈ 0.58). Although the descriptive Sharpe ratio 
of the portfolio (0.443) exceeds that of the Ibovespa (0.039), the difference is not statistically 
significant under the Jobson-Korkie test (Z = 1.427; p = 0.154). These findings advance the debate 
on semi-strong efficiency in the Brazilian market and carry direct implications for active strategy 
pricing and disclosure. 
Keywords: return decomposition; Jensen alpha; risk factors; hybrid portfolio; backtesting; value 
investing; Brazilian stock market. 
1. INTRODUCTION 
The Brazilian asset management industry has seen growing volumes channeled into 
strategies that combine fundamental analysis with technical analysis — the so-called hybrid 
portfolios. Equity funds and managed accounts frequently justify performance fees on the alleged 
capacity of such strategies to identify undervalued assets and optimize position timing. Yet an 
empirical question remains insufficiently answered in the domestic literature: where, in fact, does 
the return of these strategies come from? Does it reflect genuine alpha generation — evidence of 
managerial skill or market inefficiency — or is it merely a reflection of exposure to systematic risk 
factors that investors could replicate at substantially lower cost through passive instruments? 
The international factor investing literature, inaugurated by the seminal work of Fama and 
French (1992, 1993) and consolidated by Carhart (1997), demonstrated that a large fraction of 

=== page 2 ===
active strategy returns can be decomposed into exposures to systematic factors — value (HML), 
size (SMB), and momentum (WML — winners-minus-losers). In the Brazilian market, Málaga 
and Securato (2004) and Mussa et al. (2012) documented the presence of these risk premia at B3, 
but the application of this framework to concrete hybrid portfolios — combining a fundamentalist 
screen with technical timing — remains underexplored. Machado and Medeiros (2011) and 
Yoshinaga et al. (2012) advanced the investigation of anomalies and return factors in the Brazilian 
market without, however, focusing specifically on this intersection. 
This paper empirically investigates the return decomposition of a hybrid portfolio 
constructed and operated at B3 between 2000 and 2025. The research question is not whether the 
hybrid model outperforms buy-and-hold — a topic extensively debated in the literature — but 
rather: what is the relative contribution of each component to the portfolio's return and risk? Are 
the observed returns explained by exposure to known systematic factors, or is there genuine alpha 
generation beyond those factors? 
To address this question, we adopt a two-layer methodology. In the first layer, we build the 
portfolio through a rigorous fundamentalist screen and operate it with a simple moving average 
crossover system, evaluating performance through descriptive risk-return metrics. In the second 
layer — which is central to the paper's argument — we estimate CAPM and Fama-French three- 
and four-factor models to decompose return sources and test for alpha generation. The analysis is 
complemented by sensitivity tests using multiple parameterizations and by sub-period analysis, 
mitigating the data snooping concerns discussed by Sullivan, Timmermann, and White (1999). 
The results reveal a positive and statistically significant Jensen alpha of 1.11% per month 
(p = 0.005) — evidence that challenges the semi-strong efficiency hypothesis in the Brazilian 
market. The four-factor model with momentum confirms the robustness of the finding: alpha of 
0.98% per month (p = 0.009), with WML non-significant (p = 0.409). Unexpectedly, the HML 
factor is not significant, contradicting the expectation that the fundamentalist screen would 
primarily load on the value premium. These findings have direct implications for three audiences: 
researchers interested in Brazilian market efficiency and the Adaptive Markets Hypothesis (Lo, 
2004); investors considering hybrid portfolios as an alternative to passive investing; and regulators 
engaged in the debate on transparency and the appropriateness of performance fees. 
The paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents the theoretical framework; Section 3 
describes the methodology; Section 4 presents and discusses the results; and Section 5 offers 
concluding remarks, limitations, and directions for future research. 
2. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK 
2.1 Return Decomposition and Factor Investing 
The framework for decomposing returns through risk factors represents one of the most 
significant advances in modern financial theory. Fama and French (1992, 1993) demonstrated that 
a security's expected return is not explained solely by market risk — as proposed by the CAPM of 
Sharpe (1964) and Lintner (1965) — but also by factors related to firm size (SMB — small minus 
big) and book-to-market multiple (HML — high minus low). Carhart (1997) incorporated the 
momentum factor, and Fama and French (2015) expanded the model to five factors, adding 
profitability (RMW — robust minus weak) and investment (CMA — conservative minus 

=== page 3 ===
aggressive). As an alternative, Hou, Xue, and Zhang (2015) propose the q-factor model, which 
offers additional theoretical grounding for the interpretation of anomalous returns. 
The central implication of this framework for the present study is that asset selection 
strategies that systematically favor smaller-capitalization firms with low valuation multiples tend 
to deliver returns above the market not necessarily through active skill, but through exposure to 
known and replicable risk premia. Identifying this distinction is fundamental to evaluating the true 
value added by any active strategy (Jensen, 1968). In the Brazilian market, Málaga and Securato 
(2004) were the first to systematically test the three-factor model at B3, finding evidence of the 
relevance of HML and SMB factors. Mussa et al. (2012) confirmed these results with broader 
samples, and Machado and Medeiros (2011) documented the momentum effect at B3. 
2.2 Efficient Market Hypothesis and the Adaptive Perspective 
The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH), systematized by Fama (1970), postulates that 
asset prices fully reflect all available information. In its semi-strong form, the hypothesis implies 
that strategies based on publicly available information — including fundamental data and price 
history — would not consistently generate abnormal returns. The presence of significant alpha, as 
documented in this study, constitutes evidence that challenges this hypothesis in the Brazilian 
market, although a single study based on a specific portfolio is insufficient to refute it in a general 
sense. 
The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis (AMH), proposed by Lo (2004) and developed in Lo 
(2017), offers a complementary dynamic perspective: efficiency varies over time in response to 
changes in the competitive, regulatory, and behavioral environment. This perspective justifies the 
sub-period analysis adopted in this study — technical timing strategies may be effective in specific 
regimes of pronounced trends without being effective in general — and aligns with the finding 
that alpha tends to concentrate in the earlier sub-periods of the sample, when the Brazilian market 
was less developed. Ang (2014) and Ilmanen (2011) discuss, respectively, the temporal evolution 
of risk factors and the dynamic coexistence of risk premia and inefficiencies in financial markets. 
2.3 Fundamental Analysis as a Factor Strategy 
Fundamental analysis, in its value investing approach, rests on the premise that the market 
misprices assets whose intrinsic value diverges from the market quotation (Graham; Dodd, 1934). 
The empirical systematization by Fama and French (1992) demonstrated that this divergence can 
partly reflect exposure to the HML factor — a compensatory risk premium for structural 
characteristics of value firms. By systematically selecting companies with low P/E and P/BV close 
to book value, the fundamentalist screen builds a portfolio with potential factor loading on HML 
and, given the profile of the selected companies, potentially on SMB as well. 
However, as documented by Lakonishok, Shleifer, and Vishny (1994), part of the return 
from value strategies may reflect not only systematic risk but also behavioral mispricing — leaving 
room for genuine alpha even after factor controls. Almeida and Sales (2020) and Marques et al. 
(2015) document this effect in the Brazilian context. Additionally, when the screen incorporates 
quality criteria (high ROE and Net Margin), the resulting portfolio approaches the quality investing 
profile — historically exhibiting low correlation with pure HML (Asness; Frazzini; Pedersen, 
2019) — which may partly explain the non-significance of HML documented in this study and 
discussed in detail in Section 4.

2.4 Technical Analysis, Timing, and the Data Snooping Problem 
Technical analysis based on moving averages operates under the premise that price patterns 
are informative about future price movements. Wagnitz, Partyka, and Lana (2021) document the 
use of moving-average crossovers as trend trackers in the Brazilian market, while Wolwacz (2010) 
provides the theoretical basis for their application in specific setups. The effectiveness of these 
strategies, however, is heterogeneous: Da Silva Fernandes et al. (2015) find mixed evidence on 
candlestick patterns, and Carvalho, Barboza, and Fiorucci (2020) report significant sensitivity of 
results to the choice of parameters. 
Brock, Lakonishok, and LeBaron (1992) provided early evidence that technical trading 
rules have predictive power, although subsequent studies have attributed part of this effect to data 
snooping and regime changes. The data snooping problem — identified by Sullivan, Timmermann, 
and White (1999) as the primary bias in backtesting of technical strategies — is mitigated in this 
study by grounding the main parameter choice (SMA 9-46) in the published literature (Wolwacz, 
2010), by conducting sensitivity tests with alternative configurations, and by evaluating the 
stability of results across distinct sub-periods. 
2.5 Performance Fees and Genuine Alpha 
The most practically relevant implication of the factorial return decomposition concerns 
the appropriateness of charging performance fees on active strategies. Sharpe (1991) demonstrated 
that, in aggregate, active managers do not outperform the market after costs. The smart beta 
literature — especially Asness, Frazzini, and Pedersen (2019) and Israel and Moskowitz (2013) 
— demonstrated that a large fraction of active strategy returns can be replicated through systematic 
factor exposures at substantially lower cost. 
The existence of positive and significant alpha changes this argument: if part of the return 
is not replicable by systematic factors, there is a theoretical basis for charging performance fees 
on that excess. The normative argument shifts from 'managers should not charge performance fees 
for factor exposure' to 'managers should be transparent about what fraction of the return is genuine 
alpha versus replicable factor exposure.' Fung and Hsieh (2004) propose a methodology for this 
decomposition, applicable to the context analyzed here. In the Brazilian market, this question is 
especially relevant given the historically high management and performance fee environment. 
3. METHODOLOGY 
The study adopts a quantitative methodology organized into two complementary analytical 
layers. In the first, we conduct a structured backtesting to assess the descriptive performance of 
the hybrid portfolio. In the second, we estimate factorial econometric models to decompose return 
sources and test for alpha generation. The time horizon spans from January 2000 to December 
2025 — a period encompassing the dot-com crisis (2000–2002), the global financial crisis (2008–
2009), the Brazilian recession (2015–2016), and the pandemic shock (2020) — providing a 
rigorous test for any investment strategy. 
3.1 Portfolio Construction: Fundamentalist Screen 
The first layer of the strategy consists of constructing a portfolio through rigorous 
fundamental criteria, applied using ex-ante available data to mitigate look-ahead bias. Data were 

=== page 5 ===
sourced from the StatusInvest platform, with the following selection parameters: (i) P/E (price-to-
earnings) ratio ≤ 15 — focus on firms not overvalued by the market; (ii) ROE (return on equity) > 
15% — efficiency in generating profit relative to shareholders' equity; (iii) Net Margin > 15% — 
capacity to convert revenue into profit; (iv) Current Ratio ≥ 1 — short-term solvency; and (v) 
P/BV (price-to-book value) between 0.5 and 1.5 — proximity to book value. 
The fundamentalist screen was applied once, at the beginning of the analysis period, 
generating a fixed set of 13 assets comprising the portfolio throughout the entire time window — 
without periodic rebalancing of composition. This choice was motivated by the objective of 
isolating the effect of the technical timing signal on a stable set of fundamental assets. We 
acknowledge, however, that this decision implies a relevant limitation: as the fundamentals of the 
companies change over the 25-year horizon, the portfolio composition may diverge from what the 
screen would indicate at later dates. In a real implementation, the portfolio manager would 
periodically update the selection — an aspect that the present design does not capture (see Section 
5). 
Historical price data used in the backtesting are adjusted for dividends and corporate 
events, mitigating distortions caused by corporate actions. Simulations were conducted on the 
TradingView platform with a theoretical initial capital of BRL 1,000,000.00 per asset. Transaction 
costs (brokerage, exchange fees, and bid-ask spread) were not included in the main simulation — 
a limitation discussed in Section 5. The reported results represent the simple average of the 13 
individually simulated assets, not a portfolio with optimal weighting or managed allocation.

3.2 Operationalization: Technical Moving Average System 

=== page 6 ===
Applied to the fundamentalist portfolio, a trend-tracking algorithm based on a simple 
moving average (SMA) crossover was implemented, with the baseline configuration defined a 
priori and grounded in the literature (Wolwacz, 2010): Short Average — SMA of 9 periods; Long 
Average — SMA of 46 periods; Buy Signal when SMA(9) crosses above SMA(46); Sell Signal 
when SMA(9) crosses below SMA(46). No fixed stop-losses were used; exits were determined 
exclusively by the opposite technical signal. Returns were calculated as total return, adjusted for 
dividends. Monthly time series were constructed from daily returns on operations, consolidated 
into monthly windows for compatibility with the risk factors from NEFIN/FEA-USP, which are 
made available at monthly frequency. 
3.3 Descriptive Risk-Return Metrics 
The descriptive metrics of the first analytical layer include: (i) Total Net Profit; (ii) 
Maximum Drawdown — the largest percentage loss from peak to trough; (iii) Sharpe ratio (Sharpe, 
1966); (iv) Sortino ratio (Sortino; Van der Meer, 1991); and (v) Profit Factor — the ratio of total 
gross profit to total gross loss across all operations. All metrics are calculated for the hybrid 
portfolio, for the buy-and-hold strategy on the same assets, and for the Ibovespa as the market 
benchmark. Formal comparison of Sharpe ratios is conducted using the Jobson and Korkie (1981) 
procedure with the Memmel (2003) correction. 
3.4 Econometric Models for Factor Decomposition 
The second analytical layer — central to the paper's argument — consists of estimating 
factor models to decompose the return sources of the hybrid portfolio. Monthly simple returns 
adjusted for dividends were used for the period from January 2000 to December 2025 (T = 300 
observations). The risk-free rate corresponds to the monthly average of the SELIC rate. SMB and 
HML factors for the Brazilian market were obtained from NEFIN/FEA-USP. Regressions were 
estimated by OLS (ordinary least squares — MQO in the original Portuguese) with 
heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors (White, 1980). 
The CAPM model (Jensen, 1968) is specified as: 
Rp − Rf = α + β (Rm − Rf) + ε 
The Fama-French Three-Factor Model (1993) is specified as: 
Rp − Rf = α + βm (Rm − Rf) + βs SMB + βh HML + ε 
where Rp is the monthly portfolio return, Rf is the risk-free rate, Rm is the Ibovespa return, 
SMB is the size premium, and HML is the value premium, all constructed by NEFIN. The 
coefficient α — Jensen's alpha — measures the generation of abnormal return not explained by the 
systematic factors. Additionally, a four-factor model including the momentum factor (WML — 
winners minus losers) is estimated following Carhart (1997), as a robustness test. 
3.5 Robustness Tests 
To mitigate the risk of data snooping (Sullivan; Timmermann; White, 1999), sensitivity 
tests were conducted using three alternative SMA configurations in addition to the baseline (9-46): 
SMA 5-20 (more reactive), SMA 12-26 (classic MACD configuration), and SMA 20-50 (more 
conservative). The selection of these alternatives reflects combinations widely referenced in the 
technical analysis literature, avoiding ad hoc parameter selection. The analysis was segmented into 

=== page 7 ===
three sub-periods — 2000–2008, 2009–2016, and 2017–2025 — corresponding to distinct market 
regimes, to evaluate the temporal stability of the results. 
4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
The presentation of results follows the proposed analytical order: we begin with the factor 
decomposition — the central finding of the paper — and then contextualize with the descriptive 
risk-return metrics and robustness tests.

The Fama-French three-factor model exhibits greater explanatory power than the CAPM 
(R² = 0.28 vs. 0.23). The central finding of the paper is unambiguous: Jensen's alpha remains 
positive and statistically significant (α = 1.11% p.m.; t = 2.78; p = 0.005), demonstrating that the 
three systematic factors are insufficient to fully explain the portfolio's return. The market beta 
declines to 0.5823 (t = 7.56; p = 0.000), reinforcing the defensive nature of the strategy. 
The SMB factor is positive and highly significant (βs = 0.3808; t = 4.11; p = 0.000), 
confirming that the fundamentalist screen systematically constructs a portfolio with exposure to 
smaller-capitalization firms — an expected result, given that companies with P/BV close to book 
value and P/E ≤ 15 tend to have lower relative market capitalization. This pattern is consistent with 
the findings documented for the Brazilian market by Málaga and Securato (2004) and Mussa et al. 
(2012). 
The most unexpected and analytically significant result is the statistical non-significance 
of the HML factor (βh = 0.1266; t = 0.61; p = 0.544). Although positive, the coefficient is of modest 
magnitude and insufficient statistical precision to distinguish it from zero. This evidence 
contradicts the expectation that a screen with explicit value criteria — P/E ≤ 15 and P/BV between 
0.5 and 1.5 — would primarily load on the value premium. Three explanatory mechanisms, not 
mutually exclusive, merit consideration: 
(a) Dominance of quality over value: the criteria of ROE > 15% and Net Margin > 15% 
select profitable and efficient firms — a profile that Asness, Frazzini, and Pedersen (2019) call 
quality investing. Quality portfolios historically exhibit low correlation with pure HML: highly 
profitable firms tend to trade at a market premium, compressing their effective book-to-market 
and dissociating them from the value factor. 
(b) Sectoral specificity and the construction of Brazilian HML: the HML factor from 
NEFIN is estimated over the entire population of B3 stocks. The portfolio analyzed here, 
concentrated in 13 assets from specific sectors (utilities, real estate, retail, and energy), may exhibit 
low covariance with the aggregate HML even if the individual multiples of its constituents are 
favorable. Yoshinaga et al. (2012) document the temporal instability of the value premium in 
Brazil, reinforcing that HML exposure can vary substantially with the sectoral composition of the 
portfolio. 
(c) Macroeconomic regimes adverse to value: the 2000–2025 horizon includes episodes 
in which value stocks suffered pronounced reversals in Brazil — notably between 2015 and 2018 
— diluting the expected factor loading on HML. Ang (2014) systematically demonstrates that 
factor premia are sensitive to the prevailing economic regime, which may attenuate HML 
significance in long samples spanning mixed regimes. 
The persistence of alpha in the four-factor model with momentum (α = 0.98% p.m.; t = 
2.613; p = 0.009; βm = 0.6134; p = 0.000; βs = 0.4059; p = 0.0004; βh = 0.1330; p = 0.532; βw = 
0.1062; p = 0.409; R² = 0.284) reinforces the robustness of the finding. The momentum factor 
(WML) is not statistically significant (p = 0.409), indicating that the strategy does not 
systematically capture serial return autocorrelation. Taken together, the results demonstrate that 
the identified alpha is not captured by any of the four investigated factors. The detailed results of 
the four-factor model are reported in Table 3.

Regarding the possible sources of residual alpha, the literature points to several non-
mutually-exclusive candidates: (a) a liquidity premium, given that several portfolio assets have 
average daily trading volume below the B3 median (Amihud, 2002); (b) exposure to quality factors 
not captured by pure HML (Asness; Frazzini; Pedersen, 2019); (c) persistent behavioral

Almeida and Sales (2020); and (d) timing skill of the technical component in regimes of 
pronounced trends, a hypothesis particularly plausible in the earlier sub-periods of the sample, 
when the Brazilian market was less developed and liquid.

Ibovespa — a descriptively large difference. The formal Jobson and Korkie (1981) test with the 
Memmel (2003) correction yields a Z-statistic of 1.427 (p = 0.154), not allowing rejection of the 
null hypothesis of equal Sharpe ratios at the 5% level. This result, however, must be interpreted 
with caution: Lo (2002) demonstrates that Sharpe ratio comparison tests have limited statistical 
power in samples of moderate size when the difference is of intermediate magnitude. The absence 
of statistical significance does not imply equality of Sharpe ratios in practice — it merely indicates 
that the available evidence is insufficient to assert it at the conventional confidence level. 
The average drawdown of the hybrid portfolio (25.49%) is substantially higher than the 
benchmark's (10.84%). This result represents the concrete cost of the technical timing component: 
the moving average system introduces opportunity costs through frequent entries and exits 
(whipsaws) and amplifies losses during periods of sideways market movement. In practical terms, 
the investor who adopts the hybrid portfolio bears, on average, interim losses more than twice 
those of the Ibovespa — a relevant cost that must be weighed against the identified alpha.

In none of the four tested configurations did the hybrid portfolio outperform the passive 
strategy in terms of Sharpe ratio or drawdown. The consistency of this result across different 
parameterizations strengthens the robustness of the conclusions and reduces the probability that 
the findings are artifacts of a specific parameter choice. It is notable, however, that the result is not 
insensitive to the choice: the most reactive configuration (SMA 5-20) significantly deteriorates 
performance (7 negative assets versus 5 in the baseline configuration), while slower configurations 
yield intermediate results — a pattern consistent with the hypothesis that short-term trend-
following systems generate more whipsaws in low-trend markets. 
The sub-period analysis reveals a pattern consistent with the Adaptive Markets Hypothesis 
(Lo, 2004; 2017): positive alpha tends to concentrate in the earlier sub-periods of the sample 
(2000–2008 and, to a lesser degree, 2009–2016), with a reduction in more recent years (2017–
2025), when the Brazilian market gained greater sophistication and liquidity. This result is 
consistent with the theoretical expectation that inefficiencies tend to be arbitraged over time as 
more market participants identify them (Ilmanen, 2011). The deterioration of alpha over time is 
equally compatible with the hypothesis that the technical component is more effective in regimes 
of pronounced trends — more common in the 2000–2010 period — and less effective in more 
mature markets. 
4.5 Implications for the Asset Management Industry: Genuine Alpha and Transparency 
The results have direct implications for the Brazilian asset management industry. The 
evidence of positive and significant alpha (1.11% p.m.; p = 0.005) provides an economic basis for 
charging performance fees on that excess — unlike the case in which returns would be fully 
explainable by factors replicable at low cost. 
However, the alpha comes accompanied by an average drawdown 2.35 times that of the 
benchmark. Fung and Hsieh (2004) demonstrate that this pattern — positive alpha with elevated 
drawdown — is characteristic of strategies with exposure to tail risks not captured by linear factor 
models. The non-significance of HML implies that the return is not replicable by a simple value 
ETF, which, in principle, strengthens the case for active management. This, however, demands a 

=== page 13 ===
symmetrical obligation of transparency: the manager must demonstrate to the investor what 
fraction of the return is genuine alpha, what fraction is SMB exposure (partially replicable), and 
what fraction is compensation for the additional drawdown. 
5. CONCLUDING REMARKS 
5.1 Summary of Results 
This paper investigated the decomposition of the return sources of a hybrid portfolio that 
combines a fundamentalist screen with a moving average crossover technical system, operated at 
B3 between January 2000 and December 2025. The central conclusion is unequivocal: the 
portfolio generated a Jensen alpha of 1.11% per month (p = 0.005) in the Fama-French three-factor 
model. The four-factor model with momentum confirms the robustness of the finding: alpha of 
0.98% per month (p = 0.009), with WML non-significant (p = 0.409). The SMB factor was highly 
significant (βs = 0.3808; p = 0.000), while HML did not reach statistical significance (βh = 0.1266; 
p = 0.544). The portfolio exhibits a defensive profile (market beta ≈ 0.58) and a descriptive Sharpe 
ratio superior to the Ibovespa, although the difference is not statistically significant under the 
Jobson-Korkie test. 
The paper's contributions are twofold. The first is to demonstrate that the three-factor 
model is insufficient to fully explain the returns of the analyzed hybrid portfolio — there exists a 
component of genuine alpha not captured by the investigated systematic factors. The second is to 
show that the non-significance of HML for a portfolio with explicit value criteria suggests that 
factor exposure structure in the Brazilian market can diverge substantially from the international 
pattern when quality criteria override value criteria — a finding with relevant implications for the 
domestic finance research agenda. 
5.2 Limitations 
This study presents relevant limitations that must be considered when interpreting the 
results. 
Small sample and survivorship bias: the portfolio consists of only 13 assets. The absence 
of companies that satisfied the filter at the time of selection but subsequently went bankrupt, were 
delisted, or suffered severe deterioration introduces survivorship bias and tends to systematically 
overstate results. Generalizing the findings to other fundamentalist portfolios requires caution. 
Absence of rebalancing and residual look-ahead bias: the fundamentalist screen was 
applied only once. In a real implementation, the manager would periodically update the selection 
based on the most recent financial statements. The absence of rebalancing implies that, beyond a 
certain point, portfolio assets may no longer meet the screen's criteria. Additionally, the effective 
availability of fundamental data at the start of 2000 for all assets was not verified in detail, which 
may introduce residual look-ahead bias. 
Data snooping and potential technical overfitting: the SMA 9-46 parameterization was 
selected based on Wolwacz (2010), but this does not fully eliminate the risk of data snooping. The 
sensitivity tests with alternative configurations mitigate, but do not eliminate, this concern. 
Sullivan, Timmermann, and White (1999) argue that only universe data snooping tests provide 
complete control. 

=== page 14 ===
Absence of transaction costs: the simulation does not incorporate brokerage fees, 
exchange fees, taxes, or bid-ask spreads. Technical timing strategies generate a high number of 
operations, making the impact of these costs potentially significant. Studies of technical trading in 
emerging markets suggest total costs of 0.5% to 1.0% per operation at B3, which could materially 
reduce the identified alpha on a net basis. 
Aggregation method and correlation effects: the reported results represent the simple 
average of the 13 assets, not the return of a portfolio with dynamic allocation or capitalization 
weighting. This methodology understates the importance of inter-asset correlation effects in total 
risk management. 
Limitations of factor models: the R² of the estimated models (0.23 to 0.28) indicates that 
a substantial fraction of return variance is not explained by the investigated factors. The Fama and 
French (2015) five-factor model or the Hou, Xue, and Zhang (2015) q-factor model could provide 
additional explanatory power, capturing dimensions related to quality, profitability, and 
investment. 
5.3 Future Research Agenda 
Based on the limitations identified and the study's findings, the priority future research 
agenda includes: (i) the investigation of the non-significance of HML in broader samples of 
fundamentalist portfolios in Brazil, to determine whether this finding is robust or specific to this 
configuration; (ii) the application of the Fama and French (2015) five-factor model — 
incorporating profitability (RMW) and investment (CMA) — which may capture dimensions of 
the return related to the quality profile of the selected companies; (iii) estimation with periodic 
rebalancing of the fundamentalist screen and incorporation of realistic transaction costs, bringing 
the backtesting closer to a practical implementation; and (iv) testing the Hou, Xue, and Zhang 
(2015) q-factor model as an alternative to the Fama-French framework. The frontier between 
genuinely differentiated active management and mere replicable factor exposure remains a central 
question in contemporary financial science — and especially urgent for a market like Brazil, 
undergoing an accelerating process of institutional maturation and growing competition for capital.
