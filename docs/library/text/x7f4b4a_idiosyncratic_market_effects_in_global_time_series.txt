Idiosyncratic Market Effects in Global Time Series Momentum
Andrew Butler, Maciej Zawadzki, Adam Butler
ReSolve Asset Management
Abstract
Time series momentum is a pervasive explanatory factor in financial markets whereby a market’s recent
historical average return positively predicts future returns. Recent applications of statistical learning augment
the traditional linear model definition with a more descriptive global nonlinear factor model. We extend the
work of Moskowitz et al. [47] and demonstrate that models fit at the individual market level exhibit response
curves that are distinct from the global factor model. We highlight the heterogeneity of forward market
returns as a function of time series momentum and provide a framework that enables each feature-market
pair to express its unique prediction behaviour. Empirical results demonstrate that, despite substantially
smaller sample sizes, fitting nonlinear models to individual markets is a robust and often overlooked modelling
approach that can result in a marginal improvement in out-of-sample performance.
Keywords: Time Series Momentum, Quantitative Finance, Statistical Learning, Regression
1. Introduction
Forecasting global financial markets is a challenging problem in quantitative finance. The efficient market
hypothesis posits that the sum total of investor decisions leads to a rational and complete price discovery.
Under this hypothesis, all currently available information is immediately reflected in the asset price [43]. That
is to say, asset prices follow a martingale, whose future variations are unpredictable given current information.
In contrast, many academics and practitioners reject the hypothesis that financial agents act in a perfectly
rational manner with no friction. Instead, factors such as investor biases [31], social influence [36] and limits
to arbitrage [51] can result in pricing inefficiencies which can persist for a meaningful period of time.
The use of market ‘factors’ to forecast and explain asset returns has a long and established history in the
financial literature. Notably, the capital asset pricing model (CAPM), first introduced by Treynor [56], Sharpe
[50] and Lintner [41], models stock returns as a linear function of the stock’s sensitivity to the corresponding
equity market. The seminal work of Fama and French [22, 23, 24, 25] expands upon CAPM by introducing
other explanatory factors such as market capitalization, value and profitability, in order to forecast stock
returns.
The observation that asset returns generally exhibit non-zero autocorrelation has also been documented
in the financial literature and suggests that asset prices do not follow a perfect martingale process. For
example, the generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) model proposed by Engle
[21] and Bollerslev [9] demonstrates that the absolute value of returns exhibits long-memory effects and other
‘stylized facts’ common to financial time series [10, 11]. Moreover, the application of general time series
analysis techniques, such as autoregressive and moving average models, has demonstrated positive predictive
power for forecasting asset mean returns (see for example [13, 16, 19, 39]). Most relevant to this work,
Moskowitz et al. [46] demonstrate that the forward returns of many global futures markets can be reasonably

forecasted by the market’s average return over a 1 to 12 month period. The so-called ‘time series momentum’
factor is therefore the observation that markets generally exhibit a small, but positive autocorrelation in
returns, and is the dominant explanatory factor for Commodity Trading Advisors (CTAs) and systematic
hedge fund performance [8, 12]. Of equal importance, the slope and/or shape of the futures term structure
contains information pertaining to commodity inventory levels and hedging activity, and has been shown
to positively predict forward returns [28, 54]. Koijen et al. [37] demonstrate that the convenience yield, or
market ‘carry’, can be estimated from the spot market and futures term structure and produce predictions
that generalize across commodity and financial markets alike. To date, the time series momentum and carry
factors remain some of the strongest, most durable, and most widely studied factors in the global factor
literature (see for example [2, 3, 4, 5, 29, 30, 32, 34, 57, 44]). While this paper focuses primarily on the time
series momentum factor, the general framework and modelling approach naturally extend to other factors
such as carry, volatility, and skewness.
Traditionally, the implementation of factor strategies often assumes a simple response function between
factor features and forward expected returns. For example, the time series momentum factor, frequently
defined as the past 12-month return, has been widely studied in the context of equities [33], mutual funds
[17] and futures markets [46]. The expected return is typically modelled as a linear function of momentum,
with a slope coefficient set to 1 and no intercept term. This imposes the assumption that expected returns are
zero when the factor score is zero, and increase linearly as the factor score increases. While straightforward
to implement, this simplistic linear specification imposes strong assumptions about the relationship between
past returns (feature values) and forward expected returns.
Statistical learning methods, on the other hand, are able to capture nonlinearities and interactions that
are often overlooked by traditional linear factor models. Rather than assuming a specific linear functional
form, machine learning methods provide a data-driven approach to uncover the optimal mapping from feature
values to forward expected returns. With the rise of advanced statistical methods and data science, much of
the recent financial literature on asset pricing focuses on the use of machine learning methods to extract price
signals from noisy financial data [15, 20, 26, 35, 38, 42]. Notably, Liew and Mayster [40] explore the use of
neural networks, random forest and support vector machines to forecast the sign of liquid ETF returns. They
consider price, volume and seasonality features and demonstrate positive predictive power over time horizons
ranging from 1 to 3 months. Related, Ta et al. [55] explore the use of linear regression and support vector
machines for forecasting stock returns. More recently, Blitz et al. [7] survey a variety of machine learning
techniques including linear regression, elastic net, gradient boosting trees and neural networks for forecasting
US stock returns. They demonstrate that, in recent years, a naive machine learning implementation results
in zero net performance. Significantly positive net returns, however, are achievable by forecasting over longer
time horizons and applying efficient portfolio construction techniques.
Closely related is the work of Carver [18], who presents a binning procedure for mapping time series
momentum features to expected volatility-adjusted returns. To attenuate the potential for model overfitting,
the author recommends fitting a single response function aggregated across multiple markets, and, consistent
with the factor literature, does not recommend fitting at the individual market level. Related, Waldow
et al. [58] explore the use of time series momentum features in conjunction with logistic regression, random
forest and gradient boosting classifiers to forecast the cross-section of futures market returns. Similarly,
Schmidhuber [49] uses time series momentum features and provides a parametric model for forecasting futures
markets. The author demonstrates that, in contrast to the factor literature, market expected returns can
be modelled as a cubic polynomial of the observed trend strength, with model parameters that generalize
across markets. Most relevant to this paper is the work of Moskowitz et al. [47] who document a persistent
nonlinear relationship between time series momentum features and forward volatility-adjusted returns. The
authors explore kernel regression and neural networks, and demonstrate that nonlinear models fit across

The remainder of the paper is outlined as follows. In Section 2 we follow the work of Moskowitz et al. [47]
and present a framework for fitting regression models to forecast forward volatility-adjusted market returns.
We limit our exploration to simple linear regression and non-parametric kernel regression, acknowledging
that alternative techniques such as local regression (LOESS), neural networks, random forest and gradient
boosting regression are equally valid. In Section 3 we present motivating examples, and in Section 4 we
perform a walk-forward analysis across 50 global futures markets, spanning over 30 years of market data. In
contrast to the factor literature, where a single model is fit to generalize across a group of markets, we explore
fitting prediction models at the individual market level. In general, we confirm the findings from Schmidhuber
[49], Carver [18] and Moskowitz et al. [47] and demonstrate empirically that market forward returns are a
nonlinear function of the time series momentum feature values. Moreover, when fit in aggregate across all
markets, the response functions converge on the previously discovered linear and nonlinear model definitions
[46, 47]. However, we show that when models are fit at the individual market level, they demonstrate a
unique nonlinear response that varies across markets in both the strength and character of the signal. The
proposed framework therefore enables each feature-market pair to express its unique prediction behaviour.
This is in direct contrast to the traditional factor literature, which advocates for fitting a unified single model
that generalizes across all markets within a group and therefore borrows statistical robustness by fitting
over substantially larger sample sizes [6, 37, 46, 47]. Empirical results demonstrate that fitting nonlinear
models to individual markets is an equally robust and often overlooked modelling approach. For the time
series momentum factor explored in this paper, we demonstrate that, in aggregate, fitting to individual
markets results in improved and differentiated out-of-sample performance at the margin. From a statistical
standpoint, we find the total effect size to be small, with market mean returns that are statistically equivalent
to those produced by both the linear and nonlinear global factor models. This is largely explained by the
observation that most markets exhibit a linear forward response when feature values are within the normal
range. Nonlinear effects therefore occur primarily at extreme feature values, which are observed much less
frequently. Statistical analysis of realized market mean returns at feature extremes provides some evidence
that the market-specific nonlinear response functions represent genuine market signal and are not purely an
artifact of sampling error. Taken together, we are encouraged by the robustness of the proposed modelling
approach as it enables data-driven factor exploration in settings that require a more fine-grained individual
market perspective.

The kernel function, κb(xk(j) − xk(t)), assigns a non-negative weight to all observations, {xk(t)}T t=1, that
decreases with the distance to the observation xk(t), whereas the bandwidth controls the span of the local

and bandwidth is important as it determines the degree of nonlinearity in the model. A small bandwidth
will result in a model that closely fits the data, potentially capturing fine-grained patterns but also being
more susceptible to noise. A large bandwidth, on the other hand, will produce a smoother model that may
overlook subtle patterns.
3. Experimental Results
Experiment data: We consider an asset universe of 50 global futures markets, described in Table A1.
Daily price data is provided by Commodity Systems Inc and is available as early as January 1950 and ex-

the nearest most liquid available contract.

on 10% of the training data. We compare 4 models:
1. Global Linear: a univariate linear OLS regression model fit globally across all markets.
2. Global Nonlinear: a univariate kernel regression model fit globally across all markets.
3. Sector Nonlinear: a univariate kernel regression model fit across all markets within a sector.
4. Market Nonlinear: a univariate kernel regression model fit locally to each individual market.

3.1. Motivating examples
We begin with some examples to help illustrate the idiosyncratic behaviour of the nonlinear models in
comparison to the global linear and nonlinear factor definitions. Note that models shown below are fit using
all available training data from January 1950 through June 2026. Figure 1 plots the estimated response
curve as a function of the 12-month time series momentum factor. Recall that the Global Linear and Global
Nonlinear models are fit using all market data. As expected, the empirical linear model perfectly recovers
the traditional time series momentum factor definition where historical average returns positively predict
forward expected returns [46]. We observe that the magnitude of the slope varies slightly across forecast
horizons; weekly forecast horizons exhibit the strongest positive slope and in contrast daily horizons exhibit
the weakest positive slope. The response function of the Global Nonlinear model is provided in Figure 1b.
First, we observe that for all forecast horizons, the Global Nonlinear model exhibits a positive and monotonic
relationship for feature values in the range −2 ≤ x(t) ≤ 2 (hereafter referred to as the ‘normal range’).
This is a commonly observed effect for most model variants and helps to explain why nonlinear models
correlate strongly with their linear counterpart: the range −2 ≤ x(t) ≤ 2 spans over 95% of the time series
momentum distribution and therefore for most feature values the linear and nonlinear models will produce
similar forecasts. However, for daily and weekly forecasts, the nonlinear model exhibits a modest sigmoidal
relationship with marginally decreasing response values as feature values become strongly positive (x(t) > 3)
or strongly negative (x(t) < −3). For monthly forecasts, the response relationship begins to flatten as feature
values approach ±3 and then begins to invert as feature values exceed the extreme level | x(t) | ≥ 3.

Figure 3 plots the estimated response curve as a function of the 12-month time series momentum factor
for the Sector Nonlinear models. Note that the majority of Sector Nonlinear models exhibit traditional time

However, there are notable differences in the response function strength and character across various sectors,
particularly at extreme feature values. For example, the metal and grain sectors exhibit predominantly
linear time series momentum behaviour across the entire feature range. In contrast, currency and equity
sectors display strong mean-reversion characteristics across all forecast horizons when feature values exceed

the time series momentum feature, whereas bond predictions are notably weaker and range between ±0.10.
From an empirical perspective, it would be easy to dismiss these observed differences as mere sampling error
around a global time series momentum model. Alternatively, a causal explanation for the differences across
sectors may arise from the composition and behaviour of the market participants within each sector. Hedgers,
speculators and financial actors may respond to changes in prices with different objectives and time horizons.
For example, in Figure 2 we observe that commodity markets produce consistent trend-like behaviour, even
at price extremes, whereas financial markets mean-revert. Indeed, in commodity markets, speculative trading
and hedging, in combination with unique supply-demand dynamics, may reinforce one another, resulting in
sustained trends at price extremes. Conversely, in financial markets, policy-driven rebalancing may trade
against extended prices, resulting in a negative feedback that manifests as mean-reversion.

historical performance? In general we find that for the 12-month time series momentum feature, financial

It is difficult to determine with certainty whether the observed differences in response function strength
and character are a result of sampling error, or indicate genuine differences in market response dynamics. We
hypothesize that the differences in commodity markets’ supply and demand dynamics, as well as inventory
availability, product substitution effects, and sensitivity to seasonality may serve as reasonable candidate
explanations for the differing trend and mean-reversion behaviour observed across individual markets within
a given sector.
4. Historical Analysis
Fitting nonlinear models at the individual market level allows for the expression of idiosyncratic market
dynamics, but may be vulnerable to sampling error as a result of small sample sizes. In contrast, fitting a
single model across all markets garners robustness from increased sample sizes, but fails to capture unique
market response characteristics. In this section we test the validity of a framework that fits models at the
market level. Historical model performance is evaluated using the following experiment design: for each
market, feature and forecast horizon, models are fit using all available data prior to January 1, 1995. We
then apply walk-forward training and testing: each model is refit every year using all available data and
model predictions are applied to the next year’s out-of-sample segment. To ensure no training data overlap,
we apply a 22-day embargo period between model training and next-year predictions. The testing period is
between January 1, 1995 and June 30, 2026. Portfolios are formed by averaging the predictions across forecast
horizons. The predictions for weekly and monthly forecast horizons are smoothed using a simple 5-day and
21-day moving average, respectively. Individual market positions are formed by dividing the average market
prediction by the 40-day market volatility estimate. Portfolios are rebalanced on a daily basis, assuming
formation at the market close, and trade execution at the next-day market closing price. Results are scaled
to a volatility of 20% and are net of slippage and commission transaction costs, described in more detail in
Table A1.
4.1. 1-Month Time Series Momentum
Figure 5 reports the out-of-sample equity growth and Sharpe ratio for models fit using the 1-month time
series momentum feature. We observe that the Global Linear model exhibits a near-zero Sharpe ratio over
the entire test horizon. In contrast, the nonlinear models produce Sharpe ratios in the 0.10–0.20 range.
Moreover, for all models, the Sharpe ratios in the 1990s and early 2000s are significantly higher than Sharpe
ratios exhibited over the most recent decade; a common theme observed across all testing. Importantly, we
observe that over every evaluation decade, the models fit on individual markets exhibit the highest Sharpe
ratio. Notably, between 1995 and 2004, the Market Nonlinear model yields a Sharpe ratio of 0.57, whereas
the Global Nonlinear model and Sector Nonlinear model yield Sharpe ratios of 0.38 and 0.44, respectively.
Table 1 reports the annualized market mean returns, averaged across individual sectors and the total
portfolio, for the 1-month time series momentum models. All results are net of transaction costs. The values
in parentheses report the t-statistic from a paired t-test of the null hypothesis that the average market return
of the stated model is equal to the average market return produced by the Market Nonlinear model. First, we
note that, in aggregate, the average market return of the Market Nonlinear model is greater than the average
market return of all other models. Moreover, the effect is monotonic: models specified with finer-grained
precision result in higher average market returns. However, the effect size is small and inconsistent across
market sectors; for example in currencies and metals, the Market Nonlinear model produces the lowest mean
return. Statistically, we are unable to reject the null hypothesis that all models produce equivalent market
mean returns.

4.4. Average Time Series Momentum
Next, Figure 8 reports the out-of-sample equity growth and Sharpe ratio for models fit using an average
of the 1, 6 and 12-month time series momentum features. The average results provide clear positive evidence
that fitting nonlinear models at the individual market level is a robust and often overlooked modelling
alternative that can result in improved out-of-sample performance. We observe that over every evaluation
decade, the Market Nonlinear model produces net Sharpe ratios that are no worse than the Global Linear
and Nonlinear alternatives, and in fact over the majority of evaluation horizons produces a marginal Sharpe
ratio improvement. With the exception of the 1995-2004 decade, the relationship between model precision
and out-of-sample Sharpe ratio is not monotonic. Indeed, we observe that in the 2005-2014 period, fitting
models at the sector level actually results in a slight decrease in net Sharpe ratio in comparison to the Global
Linear and Global Nonlinear models. When averaged across all features and evaluated over the full period,
the Global Linear model produces a net Sharpe ratio of 0.54, whereas the Global Nonlinear and Sector
Nonlinear models produce net Sharpe ratios of 0.57. In contrast, the Market Nonlinear model produces a
net Sharpe ratio of 0.60 when evaluated over the full test period. The Sharpe ratio error bars in Figure 8b
indicate that this improvement is not statistically significant; but nonetheless provides evidence in support

5. Conclusion
We extended the work of Moskowitz et al. [47] and demonstrated that models fit at the individual market
level exhibit idiosyncratic forward return characteristics that are distinct from the global linear and nonlinear
time series momentum factor models. Nonlinear models were fit using non-parametric kernel regression
and provide a framework that enables each feature-market pair to express its potentially unique prediction
behaviour. A comprehensive catalogue of model response curves is provided in Appendix A and helps to
illustrate that, even within sectors, individual markets can display heterogeneous forward return behaviour.
We provided two primary empirical findings. First, financial markets, such as bonds, currencies and equities,
tend to display stronger mean-reversion characteristics when the time series momentum factor is at extreme
levels. In contrast, commodity markets, such as energies, metals, grains and softs, typically exhibit more
traditional linear time series momentum effects, on average and across the entire feature range. Second, for
commodity markets, we also observed differences in the prediction strength and response behaviour at the
individual market level. We hypothesize that the tendency for markets to display sustained trends or mean
reversion at the extremes may be governed by both economic feedback mechanisms and the participants
specific to each market.
Empirical results demonstrated that, despite substantially smaller sample sizes, fitting nonlinear models
to individual markets is a robust alternative to the traditional global modelling approach that preserves the
characteristics of the time series momentum strategy with a high degree of correlation. Historical walk-
forward simulations, spanning over 30 years of market data, indicated that, despite increased complexity,
models fit at the individual market level generalized to the out-of-sample period. Portfolio-level results
exhibited positive out-of-sample net Sharpe ratios and, on average, demonstrated that fitting models at the
individual market level has the potential to provide differentiated and improved out-of-sample performance
at the margin. In general, all nonlinear models were found to be strongly positively correlated with the
Global Linear factor model, indicating that they build upon traditional factor intuitions, while also allowing
for the expression of idiosyncratic nonlinear response characteristics.
Statistical tests of paired differences in mean returns across markets provided mixed results. When
evaluated over the entire feature range, we demonstrated that models fit at the individual market level
produce average market returns that are statistically equivalent to those generated by the grouped linear
and nonlinear models. We observed a statistically significant increase in market mean returns when out-of-
sample results were isolated to periods of extreme feature values. However, under such circumstances, all
models displayed negative expected portfolio returns; therefore, a higher-expected-value decision would be to
completely exit the market position when feature values are at extreme levels. In totality, we are unable to
claim with statistical confidence that the unique response functions displayed by individual markets represent
genuine signal and therefore recommend further testing and analysis.
This paper serves as an initial proof-of-concept showcasing the potential of machine learning techniques for
advancing factor-based investment modelling at the individual market level. There are many promising future
extensions and areas for exploration. Indeed, fitting models at the individual market level is generally robust
and enables factor discovery that requires market-level analysis, such as seasonality [52, 45] or Commitments
of Traders (COT) data [12]. Machine learning techniques may also be well suited to discovering conditional
multivariate relationships that exist globally or at the individual market level. Recent advances in deep
learning and causal inference also offer avenues for uncovering subtle factor relationships amidst market noise
[1, 27, 59]. The results suggest that machine learning can empirically derive factor relationships directly
from market data, with the potential for flexibility and specificity exceeding that of the traditional factor
modelling approach. This framework establishes a starting point for the continued exploration of machine
learning’s benefits for data-driven factor discovery in market forecasting.
